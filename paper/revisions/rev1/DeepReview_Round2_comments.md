# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper investigates DDoS detection and source-scoped mitigation in OpenFlow-based Software-Defined Networks (SDN), addressing the common deployment gap where flow-level machine learning classifiers trained on isolated public datasets fail to transfer across networks or protect legitimate traffic during live mitigation. The authors cast SDN DDoS defense as a decomposed two-stage problem—quickest change detection under a single-window false-alarm budget paired with goodput-maximizing source block-set selection under a flow-table budget and zero-collateral constraint—and implement a three-level Ryu controller architecture combining a multi-dataset flow classifier (Level 1), 19 destination-centric OpenFlow counter-delta window features (Level 2), and a calibrated logistic detector with $k$-of-$n$ plus fast-path confirmation, baseline-aware persistence-filtered attribution, and reactive port-quarantine anti-spoofing (Level 3). Evaluated on a 200-host, 25-switch Mininet/Open vSwitch testbed across nine attack types, fast-path-parity baselines, flow-ML and attribution ablations, link-capacity recalibration, and out-of-sample fresh-seed validation, the proposed system achieves the highest window F1 ($0.78$ on tuning seed 2; $0.73$ frozen and $0.78$ recalibrated on fresh seed 3) with $\approx 3.2\text{--}3.5\text{ s}$ mean detection latency and zero collateral blocks of legitimate hosts, while transparently documenting operational edge cases such as server-concentrated false positives and attribution blindness under control-plane `packet-in` saturation.

## Strengths
* **Rigorous Experimental Hygiene and Scientific Transparency:** The paper directly confronts well-known evaluation pitfalls in network security ML by demonstrating severe cross-dataset degradation between CIC-DDoS2019 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 ($0.42\text{--}0.47$ balanced accuracy), enforcing fast-path parity across baselines, conducting out-of-sample fresh-seed validation with frozen configurations, and candidly diagnosing negative results and telemetry artifacts (such as carry-over collapse and server role skew).
* **Effective Destination-Centric Feature Synthesis and Collateral-Free Scoping:** Aggregating per-switch OpenFlow counter deltas (with a counter-restart correction) into destination-centric window features and gating per-source drop rules via pre-attack peak-hold baselines ($\beta\mu_{s,d}$) and two-window persistence achieves 100% episode recall across all nine attack types while blocking zero legitimate hosts across both test seeds.
* **Clear Problem Decomposition and Diagnostic Ablations:** Separating the design constants of the confirmation state machine from the calibrated quantile thresholds and systematically ablating both the Level-1 flow classifier (showing it contributes $\approx 0.04$ F1 while Levels 2+3 drive the bulk of the performance) and the four successive versions of the attribution rule provide clear insight into where the architectural gains originate.

## Weaknesses
* **Theoretical Disconnect Between the Bit-Rate Mitigation Objective and Packet-Rate Attribution Ranking:** Please consider aligning the formal mitigation formulation in Equations (4)–(5) with the plug-in solver in Section III-E and Equation (9); whereas Equation (5) requires greedily blocking attackers with the largest offered bit-rate load ($\ell_{a,v} = \lambda_{a,v}\bar{b}_{a,v}$) and omits port quarantine $Q$ from legitimate goodput and the zero-collateral constraint, the controller selects and ranks candidates using packet-rate thresholds ($\lambda_{\text{floor}}, \lambda_{\text{rate}}$) and logistic probabilities ($r_{s,d}$) without weighting by mean packet size $\bar{b}_{s,v}$.
* **Pre-Split Label Snooping and Pooled Role Calibration Bias:** The authors might consider resolving two statistical evaluation issues: (1) removing identical feature vectors with conflicting labels across the *entire* flow datasets prior to the 80/20 split uses holdout test labels to filter ambiguous boundary flows, violating strict pre-split isolation (Reference Paper: Dos and Don'ts of Machine Learning in Computer Security, Daniel Arp et al., 2022), and (2) calibrating the window threshold $\eta$ by pooling benign windows across $\approx 97\%$ quiet clients and $1$ busy server satisfies the $0.3\%$ budget globally while inducing a $24.0\%\text{--}29.6\%$ benign window FPR on the victim server and $30\text{--}89$ false-alarm episodes per hour.
* **Fast-Path Threshold Inversion Under Link-Capacity Recalibration:** Treating the fast-path trigger $\eta_f = 0.97$ as a fixed constant while dynamically recalibrating $\eta$ causes a threshold inversion ($\eta = 0.980 > \eta_f = 0.97$) when recalibrated at the $25\text{ Mbit/s}$ test capacity, collapsing the intended 2-of-3 sequential confirmation state machine into an unbuffered 1-of-1 rule and surging false-alarm episodes from $5.8$ to $63$ per hour on Seed 3.
* **Counter-Based Attribution Blindness Under Control-Plane Saturation and Low-Rate Attacks:** While Level-2 detection incorporates `packet-in` rates, source attribution in Equation (9) relies exclusively on installed OpenFlow counters ($\lambda_{s,d}$) and hard packet-rate floors ($\ge 15\text{--}150\text{ pps}$); consequently, low-rate attackers evade attribution ($0/40$ attributed on seed 2), and volumetric floods on fresh seed 3 that saturate the $400\text{ pps}$ switch `packet-in` limit prevent flow setup, hiding 28 of 40 ICMP attackers from attribution and highlight the need for `packet-in`-based attribution or control-plane proxies (Reference Paper: FloodGuard: A DoS Attack Prevention Extension in Software-Defined Networks, Haopei Wang et al., 2015; Reference Paper: Combining OpenFlow and sFlow for an Effective and Scalable Anomaly Detection and Mitigation Mechanism on SDN Environments, K. Giotis et al., 2014).
* **Missing Sequential Change-Point Baseline (CUSUM) for the Confirmation Heuristic:** Because the paper formulates DDoS detection as quickest change detection under a false-alarm budget and notes that its 2-of-3 plus fast-path confirmation rule is a heuristic without formal optimality guarantees, the authors might consider evaluating a standard sequential CUSUM stopping rule offline on the logged window scores $s_d(t)$ to benchmark the delay-versus-false-alarm trade-off of their state machine (Reference Paper: Continuous Inspection Schemes, E. S. Page, 1954; Reference Paper: Procedures for Reacting to a Change in Distribution, G. Lorden, 1971).

## Potential Issues And Suggestions
### [Framing, Related Work & Mathematical Problem Formulation] (Pages: 1-3)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Counter-Delta Piecewise Formulation in Equation (1) vs. Re-installed and Newly Seen Flow Entries (Section III-B, Page 2):**
   Equation (1) defines the per-switch packet counter delta for flow $f$ on switch $\sigma$ at window $t$ as:
   $$x_{f,\sigma}(t) = \begin{cases} P_{f,\sigma}(t) - P_{f,\sigma}(t - 1), & P_{f,\sigma}(t) \ge P_{f,\sigma}(t - 1) \\ P_{f,\sigma}(t)\min\!\left(1, \frac{e_\sigma(t)}{\delta_{f,\sigma}(t)}\right), & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$
   The accompanying prose specifies four operational cases: normal monotonic increments, re-installed entries with restarted counters (whose count is spread over their age $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t)$), re-added entries with continuous counters (age restarts, counter does not fall), and entries seen for the first time with $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) \le e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ (counting the whole counter). A potential concern is that because the mathematical branching condition in Equation (1) tests only whether $P_{f,\sigma}(t) \ge P_{f,\sigma}(t - 1)$, several boundary behaviors described in the prose and evaluated in the Section VI-E ablation are not captured by the formula:
   - *Entries absent at poll $t-1$ (newly seen flows or entries that timed out before $t-1$ and re-appeared at $t$):* Equation (1) does not define $P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ when flow $f$ was absent from switch $\sigma$'s flow table at poll $t-1$. If $P_{f,\sigma}(t-1) = 0$ by convention, then $P_{f,\sigma}(t) \ge P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ holds trivially for every newly observed flow, routing it to the first branch ($x_{f,\sigma}(t) = P_{f,\sigma}(t)$) regardless of whether $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) \le e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ or $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) > e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ (whereas the prose and the Section VI-E rate-spike correction imply that an entry appearing with $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) > e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ is meant to be routed to the second, age-prorated branch). Conversely, if $P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ caches the last seen counter before expiration and a flow is re-installed between poll $t-1$ and poll $t$, its new age satisfies $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) \le e_\sigma(t)$, which makes $\min(1, e_\sigma(t)/\delta_{f,\sigma}(t)) = 1$ unless the entry had already been absent for at least one poll.
   - *Re-installed entries whose restarted counter exceeds the prior entry's counter:* If a flow entry had a small cumulative counter $P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ before timing out, is re-installed from scratch, and accumulates a burst $P_{f,\sigma}(t) \ge P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ by the next poll, testing only $P_{f,\sigma}(t) \ge P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ routes the flow to the first branch and erroneously subtracts the old, expired entry's counter $P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ from the new entry's counter rather than treating it as a restarted counter.
   - *Zero-age boundary ($\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) = 0$):* In OpenFlow `OFPMP_FLOW_STATS`, if duration is evaluated at $0$ when an entry is polled immediately upon installation (e.g., if integer `duration_sec` is used), the ratio $e_\sigma(t)/\delta_{f,\sigma}(t)$ in the second branch divides by zero unless guarded by the $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) \le e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ condition first or lower-bounded via $\max(\delta_{f,\sigma}(t), \epsilon)$. Explicitly incorporating prior-poll presence and the age condition $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) \le e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ into the piecewise conditions of Equation (1) would make the telemetry model self-contained and exact.

2. **Port Quarantine ($Q$) Collateral Damage, Equivalence Scope, and Candidate Ranking in the Mitigation Problem (Section III-E, Equations 4–5, Page 3):**
   There are three potential discrepancies between the formal block-set optimization problem in Equations (4)–(5) and the actual mitigation mechanics described in Sections III-E and IV:
   - *Omission of $Q$ from legitimate goodput and the zero-collateral constraint:* In Equation (4), the port-quarantine exclusion superscript $Q$ (*"$\ell^Q$ excludes traffic entering through $Q$"*) is applied only to the attacker sum $\sum_{a \in A \setminus B} \ell_{a,v}^Q$, leaving the legitimate offered loads $\ell_{s,v}$ and $\sum_{u \in L} \ell_{u,v}$ independent of $Q$, and the zero-collateral constraint in Equation (5) is written solely as $|B \cap L| = 0$. Because Section IV-C specifies that a port quarantine drops all traffic toward $d$ entering port $(\sigma, p)$ with no allow rule for the bound address $\beta(\sigma, p)$, quarantining a legitimate host's port (or a shared/NAT port, as discussed in Section VI-G) reduces that legitimate host's goodput to zero. As written in Equations (4)–(5), placing legitimate ports into $Q$ incurs no mathematical goodput penalty and violates no constraint unless $\ell_{s,v}^Q$ is also used for $s \in L$ and $Q$ is constrained not to contain ports bound to legitimate hosts (e.g., $\{\beta(\sigma, p) : (\sigma, p) \in Q\} \cap L = \emptyset$).
   - *Bit-rate minimization objective vs. logistic source-score ranking in the plug-in solver:* Below Equation (5), the text states that maximizing $\sum_{s \in L} G_{s,v}(B, Q)$ *"is equivalent to minimising the residual attack load $\sum_{a \in A \setminus B} \ell_{a,v}^Q$ under the same constraints: block the heaviest attackers first..."* First, because $\min(1, C_v / (\dots))$ in Equation (4) saturates at $1$ once total unblocked load falls at or below $C_v$, minimizing residual attack load is a *sufficient* condition for maximizing Equation (5) (and is strictly equivalent only while the bottleneck link remains overloaded). Second, blocking the heaviest attackers first to minimize residual offered bit rate $\sum_{a \in A \setminus B} \ell_{a,v}^Q$ under a rule cap requires ranking candidates by their offered *bit rate* $\ell_{s,v} = \lambda_{s,v}\bar{b}_{s,v}$, whereas the plug-in solver described immediately afterwards ranks the attributed set *"by source score (rate-rule hits first)"* (i.e., by the logistic classification probability $r_{s,d}(t)$ across 10 features and the packet-rate threshold $\lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\,\text{pps}$ rather than bit rate $\ell_{s,v}$).
   - *Combined vs. separate rule budgets:* Equation (5) places a joint budget $|B| + |Q| \le R_{\max}$ on per-source blocks and port quarantines, whereas the controller caps per-source drop rules $B_d(t)$ at $R_{\max} = 64$ and installs port-quarantine rules $Q$ independently (Algorithm 1, lines 20 and 26). While $|A| \le 40 < R_{\max} = 64$ in the evaluation so the rule cap is not reached, clarifying that ranking by $r_{s,d}$ is a confidence-based heuristic proxy when $|B_d(t)| > R_{\max}$ would keep the formulation and algorithm aligned.

3. **Unconstrained Fast-Path Threshold $\eta_f$, Missed-Episode Delay Convention, and Pooled $H_0$ Distribution in the Detection Problem (Section III-D, Equations 2–3, Pages 2–3):**
   Three aspects of the detection problem formulation in Equations (2)–(3) could be tightened to match the empirical evaluation in Section VI:
   - *Degeneration of 2-of-3 confirmation when $\eta \ge \eta_f$:* Section III-D fixes $(k, n, \eta_f) = (2, 3, 0.97)$ as design constants while solving Equation (3) for $\eta$ as the $(1 - \varepsilon)$ quantile of $s_d(t)$ under $H_0$, without imposing the ordering constraint $\eta < \eta_f$ (or defining $\eta_f$ via a stricter exceedance budget $\varepsilon_f < \varepsilon$). Whenever calibration yields $\eta \ge \eta_f$, any window satisfying $s_d(t) \ge \eta$ automatically satisfies the fast-path condition $o_d(t) \land (s_d(t) \ge \eta_f)$ in Equation (8), collapsing the 2-of-3 rule into a 1-of-1 rule (as observed empirically in Section VI-F when recalibrating at $25\,\text{Mbit/s}$ raises $\eta$ to $0.980 > \eta_f = 0.97$). Defining $\eta_f$ in Section III-D by a stricter quantile budget $\varepsilon_f < \varepsilon$ (matching how fast-path parity is already defined for the baselines in Section V-C at $0.24\,\%$) would prevent this degeneracy in the problem statement.
   - *Handling of unconfirmed episodes in Equation (2):* Equation (2) defines per-episode detection latency as $D = \min\{t\Delta \ge \tau_a : \text{confirm}_v(t) = 1\} - \tau_a$. If an attack episode of duration $T_{\text{ep}}$ ends without confirmation (which occurs for several baselines in Section VI), the set of confirming windows within $[\tau_a, \tau_a + T_{\text{ep}}]$ is empty. Explicitly restricting $t\Delta \in [\tau_a, \tau_a + T_{\text{ep}}]$ and setting $D = T_{\text{ep}}$ when the set is empty directly connects $\mathbb{E}_{\text{episodes}}[D]$ in Equation (3) to the survivorship-free penalized latency ("Pen.") reported in Table III.
   - *Victim-specific delay objective vs. destination-pooled $H_0$ constraint:* Equation (2) defines detection delay specifically at the victim $v$ ($\text{confirm}_v(t) = 1$), whereas the false-alarm constraint $\Pr(s_d(t) \ge \eta \mid H_0) \le \varepsilon$ in Equation (3) does not specify whether the probability measure under $H_0$ is conditioned on a given destination $d$ or pooled across all active destination-window pairs $(d, t)$. Explicitly noting in Equation (3) that $\Pr(\cdot \mid H_0)$ is pooled across all active destinations $d \in H$ (which is dominated by $\approx 37{,}500$ quiet client windows per session and permits a $24\text{--}30\,\%$ benign window exceedance rate on $v$, as shown in Table V) would make the mathematical origin of the server false-positive concentration immediately clear.

4. **Rate Distortion from Path-Maximum Delta Selection and Global Maximum Reply-Interval Normalization $\bar{e}(t)$ (Section III-B, Page 2):**
   In Section III-B, the per-switch delta $x_{f,\sigma}(t)$ accrues over switch $\sigma$'s local inter-reply interval $e_\sigma(t)$ (and in the second branch of Equation 1 is explicitly scaled by $e_\sigma(t)/\delta_{f,\sigma}(t)$). The controller then computes the flow delta across switches as $x_f(t) = \max_{\sigma \in \text{path}(f)} x_{f,\sigma}(t)$ and divides by the global maximum reply interval across the entire 25-switch poll, $\lambda_{s,d}(t) = \sum_{f:s\to d} x_f(t) / \bar{e}(t)$, where $\bar{e}(t) = \max_{\sigma \in S} e_\sigma(t)$. Because switch replies arrive asynchronously with up to $1.5\,\text{s}$ of polling jitter on a $\Delta = 2\,\text{s}$ cycle, this creates two potential distortions:
   - First, along a multi-hop path $\text{path}(f)$, different switches can have different elapsed intervals $e_\sigma(t)$; taking $\max_{\sigma \in \text{path}(f)} x_{f,\sigma}(t)$ on raw packet counts before normalizing by time systematically selects the switch on the path with the longest elapsed interval $e_\sigma(t)$.
   - Second, dividing every flow's packet count by the global worst-case interval $\bar{e}(t) = \max_{\sigma \in S} e_\sigma(t)$ means that a single delayed switch reply anywhere in the network (e.g., $\bar{e}(t) \approx 3.5\,\text{s}$) depresses the computed packet rates $\lambda_{s,d}(t)$ by up to $43\,\%$ even for flows traversing switches that replied on time ($e_\sigma(t) = 2.0\,\text{s}$). While all Level-2 detection methods share this normalization, downstream source attribution in Equation (9) and drop-rule monitoring in Equation (7) compare $\lambda_{s,d}(t)$ against fixed absolute packet-rate thresholds ($\lambda_{\text{floor}} = 15\,\text{pps}$, $\lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\,\text{pps}$, and $30\,\text{pps}$). Normalizing each switch's counter delta by its own inter-reply interval $e_\sigma(t)$ before taking the path maximum, or briefly explaining why global $\bar{e}(t)$ normalization does not induce false attribution misses against the hard pps thresholds during control-plane delay, would strengthen the observation model.

### [Proposed Three-Level Detector, Source Attribution & Controller State Machine] (Pages: 3-5)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **State Machine Discrepancies: Hold-Down Bound ($Q_{e_d}$) When $e_d = 0$ and Missing $\text{quiet}_d$ Resets (Section IV-A and Algorithm 1, Lines 9–24):**
   Tracing the per-destination state machine across the prose specification in Section IV-A and the pseudocode in Algorithm 1 reveals two boundary-condition discrepancies in the recovery hold-down logic:
   * **Fractional hold-down threshold $Q_{e_d}$ when `CONFIRMED` does not enter `MITIGATING` ($e_d = 0$):** Section IV-A defines the exponential hold-down window count before transitioning from `CONFIRMED` or `MITIGATING` to `RECOVERING` as $Q_{e_d} = 3 \cdot 2^{\min(e_d - 1, 3)}$, where $e_d$ is "the number of episodes on $d$ in which rules were installed (incremented on entering `MITIGATING`; never reset in our runs)" (Algorithm 1, Line 22). However, in Algorithm 1, the recovery check $\text{quiet}_d \ge Q_{e_d}$ (Line 15) executes for destinations in either `CONFIRMED` or `MITIGATING`. If a destination transitions from `NORMAL` or `SUSPECTED` to `CONFIRMED` (Line 10) on its first episode ($e_d = 0$), and no source satisfies the attribution and persistence criteria in $B_d(t)$—which occurs empirically during unattributed low-rate attacks (where 0/40 attackers are attributed, Table VIII), during spoofed floods handled exclusively via port quarantine (Line 26), during false-alarm confirmations on benign destinations, or on the initial confirmation window before the two-window persistence rule in Eq. (10) can be met—no rules are installed at Line 21. Consequently, $\text{state}_d$ remains `CONFIRMED` with $e_d = 0$. In subsequent polling cycles, Line 15 evaluates $Q_{e_d}$ at $e_d = 0$, yielding $Q_0 = 3 \cdot 2^{-1} = 1.5$ windows rather than the intended base hold-down of $3$ windows ($3, 6, 12, 24$ windows, matching the $48\text{ s}$ maximum hold-down at $\Delta = 2\text{ s}$ noted on page 5). Clamping the exponent from below at zero—i.e., $Q_{e_d} = 3 \cdot 2^{\max(0, \min(e_d - 1, 3))}$—ensures that $Q_{e_d} \ge 3$ whenever Line 15 is evaluated.
   * **Missing $\text{quiet}_d$ reset upon entering or re-entering `CONFIRMED`:** In Algorithm 1, Lines 9–18 form a mutually exclusive `if ... else if ... else` structure. When $\text{state}_d \in \{\text{CONFIRMED}, \text{MITIGATING}\}$ reaches $\text{quiet}_d \ge Q_{e_d}$ and transitions to `RECOVERING` (Line 16), $\text{quiet}_d$ is not reset. If $d$ subsequently transitions back to `CONFIRMED`—either directly from `RECOVERING` on an over window (Line 12) or from `NORMAL`/`SUSPECTED` in a later episode (Line 10)—the `else {CONFIRMED or MITIGATING}` branch (Lines 13–18) is skipped during that transition cycle, leaving $\text{quiet}_d$ at its stale value ($\ge Q_{e_d}$). If the very next polling window $t+1$ is not over ($o_d(t+1) = 0$), Line 14 sets $\text{quiet}_d \leftarrow \text{quiet}_d + 1 \ge Q_{e_d} + 1$, and Line 15 immediately returns $d$ to `RECOVERING` after just $1$ quiet window instead of waiting for $Q_{e_d}$ consecutive quiet windows. Furthermore, Eq. (8) permits $\text{confirm}_d(t) = 1$ via $\sum_{i=0}^2 o_d(t-i) \ge 2$ on a window where $o_d(t-2) = o_d(t-1) = 1$ and $o_d(t) = 0$; if confirmation triggers on such a window, Line 10 sets $\text{state}_d \leftarrow \text{CONFIRMED}$ without counting that non-over window toward $\text{quiet}_d$. Explicitly resetting $\text{quiet}_d \leftarrow 0$ whenever $\text{state}_d$ transitions to `CONFIRMED` (at Lines 10 and 12, or gating $\text{confirm}_d(t)$ on $o_d(t) = 1$) would make the state machine lifecycle exact.

2. **Two-Window Persistence Filter State ($\tilde{B}_d(t')$) at Episode Onset and Across Episode Boundaries (Section IV-B, Eq. 10, and Algorithm 1, Lines 19–20):**
   Equation (10) defines the persistence-filtered block set as $B_d(t) = (\tilde{B}_d(t) \cap \tilde{B}_d(t')) \cup (\tilde{B}_d(t) \cap R_d(t))$, where $t'$ is "the previous window in which attribution was evaluated for $d$." Both Section IV-B and Algorithm 1 (Line 19) specify that attribution is evaluated only when $\text{state}_d \in \{\text{CONFIRMED}, \text{MITIGATING}\}$ and $o_d(t) = 1$. Two boundary conditions in this specification represent potential concerns:
   * **Cross-episode state leakage of $\tilde{B}_d(t')$:** Neither Section IV-B nor Algorithm 1 specifies clearing the stored candidate set $\tilde{B}_d(t')$ when an episode ends (i.e., when $d$ exits `CONFIRMED`/`MITIGATING` into `RECOVERING` at Line 16 or returns to `NORMAL` at Line 12). If $\tilde{B}_d(t')$ is not reset to $\emptyset$ upon recovery, a legitimate source $s$ that transiently appeared in $\tilde{B}_d(t_{\text{last}})$ during the final over window of an earlier episode (so it was never blocked and is not in $R_d$) remains stored in $\tilde{B}_d(t')$. On the very first confirmed over window of a subsequent episode—arbitrarily later in time—$t'$ still points to $t_{\text{last}}$ from the prior episode, causing $s$ to be blocked immediately on a single window in the new episode and bypassing the two-window persistence requirement.
   * **Initial confirmation window delay:** Conversely, during the first attack episode on destination $d$ (where $R_d(t) = \emptyset$), if an attack is confirmed via the 2-of-3 rule, the first over window moves $d$ from `NORMAL` to `SUSPECTED` (Line 10). Because $\text{state}_d = \text{SUSPECTED} \notin \{\text{CONFIRMED}, \text{MITIGATING}\}$, Line 19 does not evaluate or cache $\tilde{B}_d$ during that first over window (and similarly, if confirmed on the first window via the fast path $s_d(t) \ge \eta_f$, $d$ moves directly from `NORMAL` to `CONFIRMED`). Consequently, on the window $t$ when $d$ first enters `CONFIRMED`, there is no prior window $t'$ in the current episode where attribution was evaluated ($\tilde{B}_d(t') = \emptyset$), forcing $\tilde{B}_d(t) \cap \tilde{B}_d(t') = \emptyset$ and preventing any new rules from being installed until at least one additional over window occurs after confirmation—unless $\tilde{B}_d(t)$ is also evaluated and cached during `SUSPECTED` over windows. Clarifying whether $\tilde{B}_d$ is cached during `SUSPECTED` windows and explicitly resetting $\tilde{B}_d(t') \leftarrow \emptyset$ upon entering `RECOVERING` (Line 16) would eliminate both ambiguities.

3. **Statistical Calibration Scope: Leave-One-Segment-Out Refitting and Pool Definition for the Source Model (Section IV-A and Section IV-D):**
   Section IV-D states that the window and source models are fitted with class-balanced logistic regression ($C=1$) on a 10-segment calibration session and that thresholds are the $(1 - \varepsilon)$ quantile of leave-one-segment-out (LOSO) probabilities on benign windows pooled over all destinations ($\varepsilon = 0.3\%$ for the window model, $\varepsilon_s = 1\%$ for the source model). Two methodological aspects of this calibration procedure warrant clarification or refinement:
   * **Scope of out-of-fold standardization and model refitting:** Section IV-A notes that $\psi(\cdot)$ standardizes features using calibration statistics. In Section IV-D, it is not specified whether the feature standardization parameters (mean and standard deviation in $\psi$) and the logistic regression weights $(w, b)$ and $(u, c)$ are refitted from scratch within each of the 10 LOSO folds (using 9 segments to fit $\psi$ and the model before predicting probabilities on the held-out 10th segment, and then refitting on all 10 segments for deployment), or whether $\psi$ and the model weights were fitted across all 10 segments prior to extracting the quantiles (which would introduce in-sample leakage into $\eta$ and $\eta_s$). Moreover, since several of the 10 segments are purely benign periods or flash crowds containing no $H_1$ windows, clarifying how the folds and sequential features (such as the EWMA $\bar{\Lambda}_d(t-1)$ and baseline $\mu_{s,d}(t)$) are handled across segment boundaries would ensure reproducibility.
   * **Pool definition for the source model threshold ($\eta_s$):** Section IV-D notes that "source decisions are only made at confirmed destinations," but leaves ambiguous whether the $(1 - \varepsilon_s)$ benign quantile for $\eta_s$ is computed over all active $(s, d)$ pairs across all benign windows in the calibration session, or only over benign $(s, d)$ pairs at destinations whose window score exceeded $\eta$ (or at server destinations).

4. **Rule-Budget Accounting ($R_{\max}$), Hard-Timeout Re-Installation, and Port-Quarantine Lifecycle in Algorithm 1 (Section IV-B, Section IV-C, and Algorithm 1, Lines 16, 20, and 26):**
   Comparing the mitigation and anti-spoofing specifications in Section III-E and Section IV-B/C with Algorithm 1 reveals three operational ambiguities:
   * **Cumulative per-destination rule budget vs. per-window cap ($R_{\max} = 64$) and expired-rule re-installation:** Equation (5) constrains total active rules by $|B| + |Q| \le R_{\max}$, and Section III-E states that the controller installs "at most $R_{\max} = 64$ rules per destination and 64 per window." Furthermore, Section IV-C states that if a drop rule expires by its $300\text{ s}$ hard timeout while the attack continues, "the source is attributed again (immediately, as a repeat offender) and the rule re-installed." In Algorithm 1, Line 20 reads: `compute B_d(t); install a rule for each new s in B_d(t) (rank, cap R_max)`. Writing "each *new* $s \in B_d(t)$" leaves ambiguous whether "new" means $s \in B_d(t)$ *without a currently active rule in the switch flow table* (which is required to re-install expired rules for persistent attackers already in $B_d$), and Line 20 caps only the newly installed rules in window $t$ without deducting active drop rules installed in earlier windows of the same episode or active port-quarantine rules $|Q|$ installed at Line 26.
   * **Decoupled port-quarantine cleanup when $d$ never enters `RECOVERING`:** Section IV-C and Figure 1 state that anti-spoofing port-quarantine rules (`in_port= p, ipv4_dst= d, drop; priority 205, hard timeout 300 s`) are installed "[i]ndependently of the destination state" (Algorithm 1, Line 26, outside the per-destination loop) and are "removed with $d$'s rules or by timeout." However, if a spoofed flood is dropped at ingress ports quickly enough (or stays small enough) that $d$ never confirms an attack, $d$ never transitions to `RECOVERING` at Line 16 to trigger rule removal, leaving the quarantined host port blocked for the full $300\text{ s}$ hard timeout; conversely, if $d$ was already in `MITIGATING` and transitions to `RECOVERING` at Line 16 immediately after a port is quarantined at Line 26, removing all of $d$'s rules at Line 16 would prematurely lift the quarantine while spoofing continues.
   * **Blind spot between `packet-in` detection and counter-based source attribution:** In Figure 1 and Section IV-B, Level-2 destination features $\phi_d(t)$ include the destination `packet-in` rate, allowing Level 3 to confirm an attack even when table misses saturate the per-switch `packet-in` limit ($400\text{ pps/switch}$). Consequently, when non-spoofed attackers ($s = \beta(\sigma, p)$) saturate the `packet-in` queue before their reactive forwarding entries can be installed, they have $\lambda_{s,d}(t) = 0 < \lambda_{\text{floor}}$ and are invisible to both Eq. Incorporating non-spoofed `packet-in` counts per $(s, d)$ pair into $\lambda_{s,d}(t)$ or $\phi_{s,d}(t)$ in Section IV-B would close this architectural gap between detection and attribution.

### [Flow-Level Data Pipeline, Testbed Design, Baselines & Cross-Dataset Evaluation] (Pages: 4-6)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Pre-Split Label-Conflict Removal, Cross-Split Feature Duplication, and Grouping Granularity in Flow-Level Data (Section V-A, Page 4)**
   - **Definitive Methodological Issue (Pre-Split Label Snooping):** Section V-A states that *"Rows whose feature vectors are identical but whose labels differ are removed over each whole dataset before splitting (this uses all labels, including those later in the holdout); identical rows with the same label are kept."* Although transparently disclosed, filtering conflicting-label rows across the entire dataset prior to the 80/20 train/test split constitutes transductive data snooping (specifically violating the pre-split isolation principle of Arp et al. [7], which is cited in Section II as the motivation for the evaluation design). In a real network deployment, a flow classifier encounters raw traffic where benign and malicious flows may project to identical coarse 7-element lifetime vectors $z_f \in \mathbb{R}^7$ (`protocol`, `duration`, `packets`, `bytes`, `packet rate`, `byte rate`, `mean packet size`)—such as single-packet UDP queries or zero-duration TCP SYN probes—without an oracle to filter out conflicting instances beforehand. Eliminating these ambiguous boundary instances from the holdout set using ground-truth test labels removes irreducible Bayes error and artificially inflates the within-dataset holdout balanced accuracies reported in Figure 2 and Table II.
   - **Potential Concern (Cross-Split Feature Duplicates and Minute-Level Grouping):** Because identical 7-feature vectors with the *same* label are retained, grouping by bidirectional 5-tuple with protocol (for CIC-DDoS2019) or by `day × capture minute × destination port` (for CSE-CIC-IDS2018) does not prevent identical 7-tuple feature vectors generated by the same attack tool or standard client queries across different 5-tuples or different capture minutes from appearing simultaneously in both the training and holdout splits. This rewards exact vector memorization—potentially explaining why K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) achieves the highest balanced accuracy on CIC-DDoS2019 ($0.982$) and why tree-based models score $>0.97$ within-dataset while failing to transfer across datasets. Furthermore, in CSE-CIC-IDS2018 (where IP addresses are absent), DoS and DDoS attack campaigns span many consecutive minutes on the same day targeting the same destination port (e.g., port 80); grouping at the granularity of individual capture minutes allows adjacent minutes ($t$ and $t+1$) of the same continuous attack episode to straddle the training and holdout sets.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Resolve label conflicts strictly within the training split while leaving the holdout split unmodified to reflect true test-time ambiguity. Additionally, report holdout balanced accuracy both with and without exact cross-split $z_f$ duplicates removed relative to the training set, and for CSE-CIC-IDS2018, consider grouping by contiguous time blocks or distinct attack sessions rather than individual capture minutes.

2. **Granularity Mismatch Between Level-1 Training Flows (5-Tuple) and Testbed Forwarding Entries (3-Tuple) (Section V-A vs. Section V-B, Page 4)**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Section V-A, the Level-1 flow classifier $g_\theta(z_f)$ is trained on flow records from CIC-DDoS2019 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018, where each flow represents a bidirectional 5-tuple transport connection (including TCP/UDP source and destination ports). In Section V-B, however, the Mininet/Open vSwitch testbed installs forwarding entries *"per (source, destination, protocol)"* with a 15 s idle timeout. Under unidirectional 3-tuple `(source, destination, protocol)` matching, all concurrent and sequential transport connections from host $s$ to destination $d$ over a given IP protocol (for instance, an `hping3` SYN or UDP flood randomizing source ports, or a web client fetching multiple HTTP pages) are aggregated into a single OpenFlow switch entry whose `duration`, `packets`, and `bytes` accumulate continuously until a 15 s idle gap occurs.
   - **Impact:** This structural domain shift between 5-tuple bidirectional connection records in training and 3-tuple unidirectional host-pair aggregates at test time has two important downstream consequences:
     1. It shifts the test-time feature distribution $z_f$ away from the training distribution, which helps explain why the `Flow-ML only` baseline misses all TCP SYN and ICMP floods (Tables IV and VI) and why Level 1 contributes only ~0.04 F1 to the overall hybrid detector.
     2. Because a source-destination pair $(s, d)$ can have at most one active forwarding entry per IP protocol at a time (at most three across TCP, UDP, and ICMP), per-pair flow-count features in Table I (`flows`, `new flows`, `packets/flow`) collapse to small protocol counts rather than connection counts, and the source-attribution corroboration rule in Section IV-B (*"$s$ opens $\ge 10$ new flows to $d$ in the window"*) cannot be triggered by a single source within a 2 s polling window unless its 3-tuple entry is repeatedly evicted and re-installed multiple times per second.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Clarify in Section V-B whether TCP/UDP forwarding rules match only on `(source, destination, protocol)` or also on L4 ports. If they match only on the 3-tuple, explicitly discuss how this aggregation affects both $z_f$ and the $\ge 10$ new-flows corroboration condition, or consider aggregating the public training datasets by `(source IP, destination IP, protocol)` over comparable time windows before training Level 1.

3. **Calibration vs. Test Victim Link Capacity Mismatch in the Primary Evaluation (Section V-B, Page 5)**
   - **Definitive Experimental Design Issue:** Section V-B discloses that the victim link capacity $C_v$ was set to 25 Mbit/s in all test sessions (seeds 2 and 3) because the single-machine Mininet emulator delivered only 6–7 kpps of the offered $\approx 11\text{ kpps}$ flood across five switch hops and failed to saturate a 50 Mbit/s link, whereas *"the calibration session had used 50 Mbit/s."* Because several Level-2 features in $\phi_d(t)$ depend on traffic dynamics sensitive to bottleneck saturation and return traffic—such as bit rate, response ratio $\rho_d = \min(B_{d\to\cdot}/\max(B_{\cdot\to d},1), 10)$, and TCP timeout/retransmission behavior when benign load (17 Mbit/s) plus flash crowds exceed 25 Mbit/s—calibrating the logistic model weights and the $(1-\varepsilon)$ threshold $\eta$ at 50 Mbit/s and testing at 25 Mbit/s introduces covariate shift under $H_0$ in Eq. (3). Although Section VI-F (Table X) later repeats calibration at 25 Mbit/s and shows that matching the link capacity raises $\eta$ from $0.844$ to $0.980$, improves F1, and restores the $0.3\%$ FPR budget on seed 3, all primary detection and mitigation evaluations in the paper (Tables III–IX) rely on the mismatched 50 Mbit/s calibration.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Clarify in Section V-B why the mismatched 50 Mbit/s calibration is retained as the primary configuration in Sections VI-B through VI-E rather than promoting the matched 25 Mbit/s calibration.

4. **Formulation, Calibration Parity, and Completeness of the Detection Baselines (Section V-C, Page 5)**
   - **Potential Concerns:**
     1. *Global vs. Per-Destination Formulation of Destination-IP Entropy:* Baseline (iii) is defined as *"destination-IP entropy [9], the normalised entropy of all traffic over destinations, alarming on the dominant destination when it drops."* First, Mousavi and St-Hilaire [9] compute destination entropy exclusively over windows of incoming `packet-in` messages arriving at the controller, whereas here it is computed over all polled switch counter rates $\Lambda_d(t)$. Second, whereas `pps per destination`, `source-IP entropy`, and `flow-ML only` produce a score for every active destination $d$ in window $t$, normalised destination entropy is a single global network-wide scalar per window that alarms only on the single dominant destination and can never trigger alarms on secondary destinations. Section V-C does not specify how this single per-window scalar is mapped to the $\approx 100$ active `(destination, window)` pairs during pooled quantile calibration ($\varepsilon = 0.3\%$ over all destination windows in Section IV-D) or window-level FPR calculation: if non-dominant destinations are assigned a non-alarming score across all windows, calibrating at a pooled $\varepsilon = 0.3\%$ across ~100 destinations permits the dominant destination (the victim server) to false-alarm in roughly $0.3\% \times 100 \approx 30\%$ of benign polling cycles.
     2. *Fast-Path Parity and Repeat-Offender Asymmetry:* Section V-C states that every baseline is given a single-window fast path whose threshold has the same benign exceedance rate on calibration ($0.24\%$) as the hybrid's $\eta_f$. However, in Eq. (8), the proposed method's single-window confirmation fires on $o_d(t) \land (s_d(t) \ge \eta_f \lor R_d(t) \neq \emptyset)$. Section V-C should explicitly clarify that $R_d(t) = \emptyset$ during all detection-only runs (`test_det`, `test_det2`, and seed 3 detection-only) so readers can verify that the proposed detector does not gain an asymmetric confirmation advantage from repeat-offender memory in detection-only comparisons.
     3. *Absence of a Sequential Change-Detection (CUSUM) Baseline:* Because the paper casts DDoS detection as quickest change detection under a false-alarm budget (Eq. 3, citing Page [20] and Lorden [21]) and notes in Section II that its 2-of-3 + fast-path rule is a heuristic alternative to CUSUM, omitting a standard CUSUM stopping rule (applied to $\Lambda_d(t)$ or $s_d(t)$) in Section V-C leaves untested whether the custom confirmation state machine improves upon standard sequential change detection. Evaluating a standard one-sided Page CUSUM stopping rule directly offline on the existing logged window telemetry $s_d(t)$ without re-running Mininet would clarify whether classical sequential accumulation achieves a better detection delay vs. episode false-alarm (`FA/h`) trade-off than the 2-of-3 + fast-path heuristic.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Clarify in Section V-C how destination-IP entropy is calibrated and evaluated across non-dominant destinations, confirm that $R_d(t) = \emptyset$ in detection-only sessions, and consider adding an offline CUSUM baseline on the logged window telemetry.

5. **Misalignment of the Pooled McNemar Test with Balanced Accuracy and Unsubstantiated Model-Selection Claims (Section VI-A, Pages 5–6)**
   - **Potential Concerns:**
     1. *Pooled Unweighted McNemar Test vs. Per-Dataset Balanced Accuracy:* In Section VI-A (Figure 2 and Table II), classifiers are evaluated and ranked using per-dataset balanced accuracy (`BA'19` and `BA'18`) to account for extreme class imbalance in the natural-ratio holdouts—specifically CIC-DDoS2019, where the 60,000-flow holdout contains only 569 benign flows ($0.95\%$ benign) vs. 59,431 attack flows, whereas CSE-CIC-IDS2018 is majority benign ($\approx 70\%$ benign). However, McNemar's test [29], [30] is applied to unweighted discordant predictions pooled across all 120,000 holdout flows. Combining two holdouts with opposite class priors into a single unweighted contingency table means the test statistic is dominated by false-positive differences on the $\approx 42{,}000$ benign flows of CSE-CIC-IDS2018 while giving less than $0.5\%$ weight to the 569 benign flows of CIC-DDoS2019 (where the 569 benign flows carry 50% of the weight in `BA'19`). Consequently, the statistical test evaluates a metric (pooled unweighted accuracy) that contradicts the selection metric in Table II (where HGB achieves higher balanced accuracy than RF and DT on both datasets). Applying class-stratified McNemar tests (evaluating discordant pairs separately on benign flows for FPR and on attack flows for recall per dataset) or paired bootstrap confidence intervals directly on per-dataset balanced accuracy would align the hypothesis test with the evaluation metric.
     2. *Cross-Dataset Transfer at a Fixed $0.5$ Threshold:* In Figure 2, HGB trained on class-balanced data from one dataset achieves balanced accuracy below random guessing ($0.421$ and $0.469$) when tested on the other dataset at the uncalibrated $p_f \ge 0.5$ threshold. Including a threshold-free ranking metric (such as ROC-AUC) alongside balanced accuracy in Figure 2 would clarify whether the cross-dataset failure is caused by threshold shift or inverted feature relationships. Furthermore, because HGB trained on both datasets still has a per-flow false-positive rate of $3.5\%$–$5.9\%$ at $p_f \ge 0.5$ (Table II), a busy benign server receiving dozens of concurrent flows per window will frequently experience $\max_f p_f \ge 0.5$, triggering both the Level-2 flow-ML aggregates (Table I) and the source corroboration condition $\text{corr}_{s,d}(t)$ (Eq. 9) under benign conditions.
     3. *Unreported Model Sizes and Hyperparameters:* Section VI-A justifies selecting HGB by stating: *"we keep HGB because it is 4–5× faster than KNN and 23× smaller."* However, Table II only reports inference latency (`µs/flow`) and omits a column for serialized model size (nor does the text state whether *"23× smaller"* is relative to KNN or RF, or why HGB is chosen over Decision Tree, which is ~15× faster at $0.2\text{–}0.3\ \mu\text{s/flow}$ with nearly identical balanced accuracy of $0.972$ and $0.947$). Key classifier hyperparameters (e.g., $k$ for KNN, number of trees/leaves/depth for RF, ET, DT, and HGB) are also omitted. Adding a model-size column to Table II and stating the classifier hyperparameters in Section V-A would ensure full reproducibility.

   - **Potential Experimental Design and Causal Attribution Concern:** In both the seed-2 ($40\text{ s}$ gaps, Table VIII) and seed-3 ($60\text{ s}$ gaps, Table VII) schedules, all nine consecutive attack episodes draw participants from the same fixed pool of $40$ bots, while installed OpenFlow drop rules match `(ipv4_src=s, ipv4_dst=d)` across all IP protocols with a $300\text{ s}$ `hard_timeout`. Under Algorithm 1 (Lines 11–18), returning from `MITIGATING` to `NORMAL` requires $Q_{e_d} = 3 \cdot 2^{\min(e_d - 1, 3)}$ consecutive quiet windows ($o_d(t) = 0$) to enter `RECOVERING` ($6\text{ s}, 12\text{ s}, 24\text{ s}, 48\text{ s}$ for $e_d \ge 1$, where $e_d$ is never reset), plus 3 additional quiet windows ($6\text{ s}$) in `RECOVERING`—requiring up to $(24 + 3) \times 2\text{ s} = 54\text{ s}$ of uninterrupted quiet windows once $e_d \ge 4$ (note that Section V-B states that $60\text{ s}$ is *"longer than the 48 s maximum hold-down"*, omitting the $6\text{ s}$ spent in `RECOVERING`). Because $24.0\%\text{--}28.3\%$ of the victim server's benign windows within $30\text{ s}$ after an attack exceed $\eta$ ($o_d(t) = 1$, Section VI-B) or residual drop-rule hits $\text{drop}_d(t) \ge 30\text{ pps}$ occur, sporadic post-attack false positives repeatedly reset `quiet_d` $\leftarrow 0$ during the inter-attack gaps and prevent rule removal:
     1. *Fresh Seed 3 Carry-Over Across $60\text{ s}$ Gaps:* Although the inter-attack gaps on seed 3 were specifically increased to $60\text{ s}$ to exceed the hold-down (Section V-B, p. 5), Table VII shows that in the `Low-rate` episode (immediately following `TCP SYN`), **`34` of the `40` attackers were still pre-blocked at onset** (`New / active: 2 / 34 of 40`), and **`7` of `24` were pre-blocked** in `Proto. switch` (`15 / 7 of 24`). While Section VI-D transparently explains that the seed-2 `Low-rate` HTTP improvement ($0.06 \to 1.00$ with $0/40$ bots attributed) is due to avoiding carry-over control-plane collapse rather than active mitigation, Section VI-C (p. 6) claims that on seed 3 *"Low-rate, re-attack and spoofed episodes recover almost fully."* In fact, just as on seed 2, the attribution layer is structurally unable to attribute low-rate bots ($25\text{--}45\text{ pps}$ per bot falls well below $\lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\text{ pps}$ and the $\varepsilon_s = 1\%$ source score threshold $\eta_s$, attributing only $2$ new bots on seed 3); the seed-3 `Low-rate` recovery ($0.07 \to 0.95$ HTTP success) is primarily an artifact of $34/40$ bots remaining blocked from the preceding `TCP SYN` flood because post-attack false alarms prevented `quiet_d` from reaching $Q_{e_d}$.
     2. *Seed 2 Carry-Over, Repeat-Offender Memory ($M = 300\text{ s}$), and Cold-Start Latency:* On seed 2 (Table VIII), even after the second attack (`TCP SYN`, where the nominal hold-down is $Q_2 = 6\text{ windows} = 12\text{ s}$), rules fail to clear across the $40\text{ s}$ gap, leaving $36/40$ `ICMP`, $23/24$ `Proto. switch`, and $26/30$ `Re-attack` bots already blocked at onset, while the unprotected baseline suffers permanent control-plane collapse from the third attack (`ICMP`) onward. Furthermore, even when rules are removed during the $60\text{ s}$ gap on seed 3, the repeat-offender memory $R_d(t)$ retains any source blocked within the last $M = 300\text{ s}$ (Section IV-A); reusing the same 40 bot IPs across sequential episodes separated by $40\text{--}60\text{ s}$ means that attackers blocked in episode $i$ remain in $R_d(t)$ when episode $i+1$ (of a different attack vector) begins, triggering immediate single-window confirmation via Eq. (8), bypassing the 2-window persistence filter in Eq. (10), and making the `"1st rule"` latencies in Tables VII and VIII reflect warm-start repeat-offender blocking rather than cold-start mitigation latency. To isolate per-attack-type detection and mitigation performance in future runs, consider assigning disjoint attacker IP pools to distinct attack episodes (reserving bot reuse specifically for the designated "re-attack" episode) or resetting controller state ($e_d, R_d(t)$) and switch flow tables between distinct scheduled attacks.
