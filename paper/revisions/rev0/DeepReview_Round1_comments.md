# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper investigates machine-learning-based DDoS detection and source-scoped mitigation in OpenFlow-based Software-Defined Networks (SDN). Motivated by the observation that flow-level classifiers trained on single public datasets suffer from severe domain shift and fail in live deployments, the authors formulate SDN DDoS defense as a quickest-change-detection problem under a false-alarm budget coupled with a goodput-maximizing, collateral-free block-set selection problem. They propose and evaluate a three-level Ryu controller architecture—combining a cross-dataset flow-level gradient-boosting model (Level 1), destination-centric OpenFlow counter-delta window features (Level 2), and a calibrated logistic state machine with baseline-aware, persistence-filtered source attribution and reactive port quarantine (Level 3)—on a 200-host, 25-switch Mininet/Open vSwitch testbed across nine attack scenarios.

## Strengths
* **Practical Problem Framing and Cross-Dataset Empirical Insights:** The paper directly confronts the operational gap between offline per-flow ML classification and live SDN defense, demonstrating empirically that single-dataset flow classifiers fail catastrophically across datasets (scoring below chance at 0.42–0.47 balanced accuracy) and that defense requires destination-centric temporal aggregation coupled with source-scoped mitigation.
* **Thoughtful Engineering of Destination Telemetry and Source Attribution:** The system incorporates practical systems mechanisms to reduce collateral damage when benign high-rate senders and attackers share edge switches, including re-installation-aware OpenFlow counter-delta corrections, pre-attack per-source traffic baselines ($\lambda_{s,d} \ge \beta \mu_{s,d}$), two-window persistence filtering, protocol-agnostic ingress drop rules, and reactive port quarantine.
* **Comprehensive Testbed Evaluation and Transparent Reporting of Limitations:** The authors evaluate end-to-end application goodput (HTTP, DNS, Ping, bulk TCP) across a 200-host topology and nine diverse attack types against four calibrated baselines, while candidly reporting negative results such as live ICMP attribution failure, high network-wide false-alarm episode rates due to the base-rate fallacy, and test-schedule tuning limitations.

## Weaknesses
* **Missing Architectural Ablation Without the Level-1 Flow Classifier:** Although the authors conclude in Sections VI-A and VII that the system's gains come from Levels 2 and 3 rather than the flow classifier, the evaluation only compares the full pipeline (Levels 1+2+3) against Level-1-only (`Flow-ML only`) and single-feature rules; the authors might consider adding an ablation baseline using only Levels 2 and 3 (the 15 OpenFlow counter window features and 8 source-pair features without Level-1 probabilities $p_f$) to verify whether the Level-1 model provides any net improvement in detection F1 or source attribution.
* **Disconnect Between Formal Mathematical Optimization and the Implemented Heuristics:** The mathematical formulations in Section III-D–E (Equations 2–4) are not rigorously connected to the implemented controller in Section IV: single-window quantile calibration ($\varepsilon = 0.3\%$) does not formally bound the multi-trigger disjunctive confirmation rule in Eq. (6), the latency lower bound $D \ge (k-1+U)\Delta$ in Eq. (4) fails to hold when single-window fast-path or repeat-offender triggers fire (as seen in the 1.2–1.3 s latencies in Table III), Eq. (3) exhibits a unit mismatch between packet rates $\lambda_{s,d}$ and bit-rate capacity $C_v$ while leaving $G_{s,v}$ uncoupled from $B_v$, and the flow-table budget $|B_v| \le R_{\max}$ is never enforced in Eq. (7) or Algorithm 1. Furthermore, because the problem is framed as quickest change detection and constrained SDN resource allocation, the authors might consider clarifying how their heuristic relates to established sequential change-point detection and constrained SDN mitigation frameworks (Reference Paper: Procedures for Reacting to a Change in Distribution, Gary Lorden, 1971; Reference Paper: Bohatei: Flexible and Elastic DDoS Defense, Seyed K. Fayaz et al., 2015).
* **Test-Schedule Data Snooping in Source Attribution Refinement:** As noted in Sections VI-D and VI-E, the source attribution rules (`v1` through `final` in Table V) were iteratively engineered while observing collateral blocks directly on the held-out `seed 2` test schedule, which constitutes adaptive data snooping (Reference Paper: Dos and Don'ts of Machine Learning in Computer Security, Daniel Arp et al., 2022). Please consider validating the frozen controller configuration on a fresh, unseen random seed (`seed 3`) or qualifying the headline claim of zero collateral blocking accordingly.
* **Confounding State Carryover Across Sequential Test Episodes and Unexplained Live ICMP Failure:** Running all nine attacks sequentially with 40 s gaps causes unmitigated control-plane collapse to persist from the third attack (ICMP) onward and allows 300 s drop rules and 48 s hold-down timers ($Q_e\Delta$) to carry over across episodes in the mitigated run, which confounds per-attack metrics (e.g., `Low-rate` UDP shows a $0.06 \to 1.00$ HTTP recovery despite installing $0/40$ rules). Additionally, please consider diagnosing the specific root cause of why live ICMP attribution blocks only $1/40$ attackers compared to $38/40$ in offline replay.
* **Positioning Relative to Multi-Stage SDN Anomaly Detection and SAVI Prior Art:** While the synthesis of components is practical, the individual building blocks—combining window-level entropy/counter features with ML and per-source mitigation, as well as port-level source address validation—build closely on established paradigms; the authors might consider expanding their discussion of prior multi-stage SDN detectors and discussing how single-IP-per-port bindings ($\beta(\text{sw}, p)$) and `(src, dst)` drop rules behave in shared-port or NAT environments (Reference Paper: Combining OpenFlow and sFlow for an Effective and Scalable Anomaly Detection and Mitigation Mechanism on SDN Environments, Kostas Giotis et al., 2014; Reference Paper: Long Short-Term Memory and Fuzzy Logic for Anomaly Detection and Mitigation in Software-Defined Network Environment, Matheus P. Novaes et al., 2020; Reference Paper: Source Address Validation Improvement (SAVI) Framework, Jianping Wu et al., 2013).

## Potential Issues And Suggestions
### [Problem Framing, Related Work, and Mathematical Formulation] (Pages: 1-2)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Uncoupled Objective Function, Dimensional Mismatch, and Unenforced Budget in the Mitigation Optimization Problem (Section III-D, Equation 3):**
   There are three potential mathematical and modeling concerns in how the block-set selection problem is formulated in Equation (3):
   $$\max_{B_v} \sum_{s \in L} G_{s,v} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{a \in A \setminus B_v} \lambda_{a,v} + \sum_{s \in L} \lambda_{s,v} \le C_v, \quad |B_v \cap L| = 0, \quad |B_v| \le R_{\max}.$$
   - *Uncoupled Objective and Hard Link-Capacity Constraint:* In the objective $\max_{B_v} \sum_{s \in L} G_{s,v}$, legitimate goodput $G_{s,v}$ is never formally defined as a function of the decision variable $B_v$ (or of the residual bottleneck load $\sum_{a \in A \setminus B_v} \lambda_{a,v}$). Furthermore, under the strict zero-collateral constraint $|B_v \cap L| = 0$ and the hard link-capacity inequality on offered rates $\sum_{a \in A \setminus B_v} \lambda_{a,v} + \sum_{s \in L} \lambda_{s,v} \le C_v$, any feasible block set $B_v$ leaves all legitimate traffic unblocked and within link capacity $C_v$. Consequently, $G_{s,v}$ is constant across all feasible $B_v$, reducing Eq. (3) to a pure feasibility problem rather than a maximization problem. Conversely, treating $C_v$ as a hard constraint on offered rates renders the problem strictly infeasible whenever $|A| > R_{\max}$ (or when attackers remain unattributed) such that blocking at most $R_{\max}$ attackers cannot bring the residual offered rate below $C_v - \sum_{s \in L} \lambda_{s,v}$. In reality, an overloaded link drops excess packets and degrades goodput (or saturates the control channel) rather than making a choice of $B_v$ mathematically invalid. Expressing $G_{s,v}(B_v)$ explicitly as the congestion-degraded goodput on a bottleneck link of capacity $C_v$ (e.g., under proportional link sharing when total offered load exceeds $C_v$, or by minimizing residual unblocked attack load) and dropping the hard capacity inequality on offered rates would make the optimization problem well-posed.
   - *Dimensional / Unit Mismatch Between $\lambda_{s,d}$ and $C_v$:* In Section III-B (Eq. 1), $\lambda_{s,d}(t)$ is defined from cumulative packet counters $P_f(t)$ as a *packet rate* in packets per second (consistent with $\lambda_{\text{floor}} = 15\text{ pps}$ and $\lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\text{ pps}$ in Section IV-B), and Section III-C explicitly notes that volumetric floods have $\sum_{a \in A} \lambda_{a,v}$ far above "$C_v$ divided by packet size." In Eq. (3), however, packet rates $\lambda_{a,v}$ and $\lambda_{s,v}$ (in pps) are summed and compared directly against the link capacity $C_v$ (measured in Mbit/s in Section V-B) without multiplying by packet size or converting to bit rates.
   - *Disconnect Between Flow-Table Budget $R_{\max}$ and the Plug-In Solver:* The Abstract, Section I, and Eq. (3) highlight the flow-table budget constraint $|B_v| \le R_{\max}$ as a core component of the formal problem statement, stating that "$B_v$ is estimated by the attribution rule of Section IV." However, neither the plug-in attribution rule in Section IV-B (Eq. 7) nor the mitigation procedure in Section IV-C (Algorithm 1, Lines 14–15) enforces a cardinality cap $R_{\max}$ or specifies a ranking criterion (e.g., selecting the top-$R_{\max}$ qualifying sources in $B_d(t)$ by descending packet rate $\lambda_{s,d}(t)$ or source score $r_{s,d}$) when $|B_d(t)| > R_{\max}$. Similarly, Eq. (3) models mitigation solely as selecting a host subset $B_v \subset H$, whereas spoofed floods are mitigated via switch-port quarantine rules (`in_port=p, ipv4_dst=d`) in Section IV-C. Incorporating a truncation/ranking step or clarifying how $R_{\max}$ and port quarantine relate to the implemented plug-in solver would resolve this discrepancy.

2. **Quickest-Change-Detection Parameterization, Calibration Alignment, and Time Indexing (Section III-D, Equation 2 and Section IV-D):**
   Equation (2) poses detection as minimizing expected detection delay over both model parameters and confirmation hyperparameters:
   $$\min_{w,b,\eta,k,n,\eta_f} \mathbb{E}[D], \quad D = \min\{t \ge \tau_a : \mathrm{confirm}_v(t) = 1\} - \tau_a \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \Pr(\mathrm{confirm}_d(t) = 1 \mid H_0) \le \varepsilon.$$
   Several aspects of this formulation present potential concerns regarding mathematical well-posedness and alignment with the calibration procedure in Section IV-D:
   - *Degeneracy Over Confirmation Hyperparameters $(k, n, \eta_f)$ and Disconnect from Parameter Fitting:* If $(\eta, \eta_f, k, n)$ are treated as free minimization variables in Eq. (2) subject only to the marginal constraint $\Pr(\mathrm{confirm}_d(t) = 1 \mid H_0) \le \varepsilon$, the optimal solution trivially collapses to $k = 1, n = 1$ (or $\eta_f = \eta$), making the $k$-of-$n$ state machine redundant because a 1-of-1 threshold calibrated to the $(1 - \varepsilon)$ quantile of $s_d(t)$ under $H_0$ already satisfies the constraint with strictly smaller delay $D$. In practice (Sections IV-A and IV-D), the controller does not jointly optimize $(w, b, \eta, k, n, \eta_f)$ to minimize $\mathbb{E}[D]$: $(k, n) = (2, 3)$ and $\eta_f = 0.97$ are fixed architectural constants, and $(w, b)$ are fitted via single-window class-balanced logistic regression.
   - *Single-Window Quantile Calibration vs. Confirmation-Level False-Alarm Constraint:* In Eq. (2) (and Section I, "under a per-window false-alarm budget"), the constraint is written on the multi-window confirmation event $\Pr(\mathrm{confirm}_d(t) = 1 \mid H_0) \le \varepsilon$ and states that "the threshold $\eta$ is set on calibration data to meet $\varepsilon$." However, in Section IV-D, $\eta$ is calibrated solely as the $(1 - \varepsilon)$ quantile of single-window leave-one-segment-out probabilities $s_d(t)$ on benign windows ($\Pr(s_d(t) \ge \eta \mid H_0) \approx \varepsilon = 0.3\%$), while $\eta_f = 0.97$ is fixed. Because Equation (6) defines confirmation as the disjunction of a 2-of-3 sequential rule over $\eta$, the fast path $s_d(t) \ge \eta_f$, and the repeat-offender condition $(s_d(t) \ge \eta \wedge R_d(t) \neq \emptyset)$—and because consecutive $2\text{ s}$ window scores are temporally autocorrelated—setting the marginal single-window exceedance rate to $\varepsilon$ does not make the composite confirmation false-alarm probability $\Pr(\mathrm{confirm}_d(t) = 1 \mid H_0)$ equal to $\varepsilon$. In addition, Eq. (2) does not specify the probability distribution over which $\mathbb{E}[D]$ is taken (e.g., averaging over a uniform polling offset $U \in (0, 1]$ or taking a worst-case supremum over $\tau_a$ as in Lorden [18]), and Section IV-D does not define what constitutes a "segment" in the "leave-one-segment-out probabilities on benign windows" procedure or how attack segments are partitioned during class-balanced logistic regression fitting to prevent temporal leakage.
   - *Discrete Window Index vs. Continuous Time Units:* Section III-B defines $t = 1, 2, \dots$ as a dimensionless discrete window index for windows of length $\Delta$, whereas $\tau_a$ in Section III-A and latency $D \ge (k - 1 + U)\Delta$ in Section III-E (Eq. 4) are measured in continuous time (seconds). Writing $D = \min\{t \ge \tau_a : \mathrm{confirm}_v(t) = 1\} - \tau_a$ in Eq. (2) directly subtracts a continuous timestamp $\tau_a$ from a discrete window index $t$. Writing the stopping condition in terms of window completion times $t\Delta \ge \tau_a$ (i.e., $D = \min\{t\Delta \ge \tau_a : \mathrm{confirm}_v(t) = 1\} - \tau_a$) would make the units of $D$ consistent between Eqs. (2) and (4).

3. **Unformalized Counter-Delta Adjustments for Re-Installed Flow Entries and Switch Indexing (Section III-B, Equation 1):**
   In Section III-B, Eq. (1) defines the per-window packet delta strictly as the plain difference:
   $$x_f(t) = \max_{\mathrm{sw} \in \mathrm{path}} \big[ P_f(t) - P_f(t-1) \big].$$
   The prose immediately following Eq. (1) states two critical exceptions: *"When an entry is re-installed, only the share of its counter that falls inside the window is counted; when an entry is re-added with continuous counters, the plain difference is used."* Because Section VI-D (Table V) credits this counter-delta correction with eliminating $6\times$ one-window rate spikes (e.g., a $104\text{ pps}$ stream measured at $629\text{ pps}$) and preventing collateral blocking of legitimate hosts, two aspects of its specification warrant clarification:
   - *Missing Mathematical Definition of Re-installation Adjustment:* First, the cumulative counter $P_f(t)$ depends on the switch $\mathrm{sw}$, which is omitted from the notation $P_{f,\mathrm{sw}}(t)$. Second, Eq. (1) does not reflect the re-installation adjustment mathematically, nor is the formula for *"the share of its counter that falls inside the window"* defined anywhere in the paper (for instance, whether $P_{f,\mathrm{sw}}(t)$ is scaled by $\min(1, \Delta / \mathrm{duration}_{f,\mathrm{sw}}(t))$ when $P_{f,\mathrm{sw}}(t-1)$ belongs to an expired rule, or how the controller distinguishes a reset re-installed entry from a continuous-counter re-added entry using OpenFlow flow statistics). Providing the explicit piecewise formula for $x_f(t)$ is necessary to make this key mechanism reproducible.
   - *Asynchronous Polling Across Switches:* Taking $\max_{\mathrm{sw} \in \mathrm{path}} [P_f(t) - P_f(t-1)]$ assumes that all switches along the path represent active transit of flow $f$ polled over identical intervals. Because the controller polls 25 switches over a window of up to $1.5\text{ s}$ (Algorithm 1, Line 1), taking the maximum across all switches on a path can systematically select whichever switch experienced the longest inter-poll interval due to polling jitter.

4. **Scope and Assumptions of the Detection Latency Lower Bound (Section III-E, Equation 4):**
   Section III-E states that *"The first window that contains the attack ends at most $\Delta$ after $\tau_a$, and $k$-of-$n$ confirmation adds $(k-1)\Delta$, so $D \ge (k - 1 + U)\Delta, \; U \in (0, 1]$."* Two unstated assumptions in this bound create an apparent contradiction with the rest of the paper:
   - *Omission of Fast-Path and Repeat-Offender Bypasses:* With the deployed parameters $\Delta = 2\text{ s}$ and $k = 2$, Eq. (4) implies $D > 2.0\text{ s}$ for all attacks. However, the proposed confirmation rule in Eq. (6) includes two single-window bypasses—the fast path ($s_d(t) \ge \eta_f$) and repeat-offender memory ($s_d(t) \ge \eta \wedge R_d(t) \neq \emptyset$)—both of which confirm an attack in the very first window ($k = 1$ effective delay, reducing the lower bound to $D \ge U\Delta \in (0, 2]\text{ s}$). Similarly, even under standard $k$-of-$n$ confirmation, if one of the $n - 1$ windows immediately preceding $\tau_a$ already had a benign fluctuation $s_d \ge \eta$, the first attack window suffices to reach $k = 2$ of $n = 3$. This explains why Table III reports mean detection latencies of $1.2\text{ s}$ (Mixed UDP+SYN) and $1.3\text{ s}$ (ICMP flood)—values strictly below the $2.0\text{ s}$ lower bound implied by Eq. (4).
   - *Sub-Window Accumulation at Small $U$:* In Eq. (4), $U\Delta \in (0, \Delta]$ represents the time between $\tau_a$ and the end of the first polling window overlapping the attack. If an attack starts infinitesimally before a poll ($U \to 0^+$), the partial window will not accumulate enough packets for $s_d(t)$ to cross $\eta$, pushing the first above-threshold window to the following poll and adding another $\Delta$. Explicitly scoping Eq. (4) to pure $k$-of-$n$ confirmation (with no pre-attack exceedances and sufficient first-window traffic to cross $\eta$) prior to introducing the fast path would resolve the apparent contradiction with Table III.

5. **Boundary Singularities and Undefined Terms in Level-2 Feature Definitions (Section III-B):**
   In the definition of the destination feature vector $\phi_d(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{19}$ and source-destination pair feature vector $\phi_{s,d}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$, several features can encounter division-by-zero or $\log(0)$ singularities during normal operation or lack explicit mathematical formulas:
   - *Normalized Source Entropy:* Defined as $H_d = -\sum_s q_s \log q_s / \log n_d$ with $q_s = \lambda_{s,d} / \Lambda_d$. Whenever a destination has only $n_d = 1$ active source in window $t$, $\log n_d = \log(1) = 0$ in the denominator, causing a $0/0$ singularity (and when $n_d = 0$, $\Lambda_d = 0$ leaves $q_s$ undefined).
   - *Response Ratio and Rate Change Features:* The response ratio $\rho_d$ (bytes from $d$ to its sources over bytes towards $d$) and the per-pair response ratio in $\phi_{s,d}(t)$ have a zero denominator if no bytes arrive at $d$ in a window (and similarly for flagged-flow/flagged-packet shares when active flows or packets are zero). Furthermore, "log rate change" in $\phi_d(t)$ and "rate jump" and "active windows" in $\phi_{s,d}(t)$ are listed by name only without algebraic formulas specifying whether "rate jump" is a difference or ratio relative to window $t-1$ (or to baseline $\mu_{s,d}$), whether "active windows" is a cumulative or sliding-window count, and how zero rates in $t-1$ are smoothed. Specifying the boundary conventions (e.g., setting $H_d = 0$ when $n_d \le 1$ and stating the smoothing constants) would ensure the feature extraction pipeline is unambiguously reproducible.

### [Proposed Three-Level Detection, Source Attribution, and Mitigation Architecture] (Pages: 2-4)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Incomplete State-Machine Transitions and Baseline-Update Deadlock in Algorithm 1 and Section IV-A:**
   In Section IV-A, the prose describes a five-state per-destination state machine consisting of `NORMAL`, `SUSPECTED`, `CONFIRMED`, `MITIGATING`, and `RECOVERING`. However, the pseudocode in Algorithm 1 omits several state transitions required to make this state machine operational, which is a potential concern for algorithmic completeness and reproducibility:
   - **Missing Transition from `RECOVERING` to `NORMAL` (Frozen Baselines):** In Line 18 of Algorithm 1, after $Q_e$ quiet windows, the controller removes the drop rules for destination $d$ and sets $\text{state}_d \leftarrow \text{RECOVERING}$, and Line 11 sets $\text{state}_d \leftarrow \text{CONFIRMED}$, but no transition in Algorithm 1 or the prose ever returns $\text{state}_d$ from `RECOVERING` back to `NORMAL`. Because Line 7 updates the per-source pre-attack baselines $\mu_{s,d}$ only when $\text{state}_d \in \{\text{NORMAL}\}$ and $s_d < \eta$, a literal implementation of Algorithm 1 permanently freezes $\mu_{s,d}$ for destination $d$ after its first attack episode ends.
   - **Omission of `SUSPECTED` and `MITIGATING` Transitions and Unscoped Quiet-Window Check:** The state `SUSPECTED` is never assigned or referenced in Algorithm 1, and although Line 13 checks whether $\text{state}_d \in \{\text{CONFIRMED}, \text{MITIGATING}\}$, no line in the pseudocode ever transitions $\text{state}_d$ from `CONFIRMED` into `MITIGATING` (e.g., after installing drop rules in Line 15). Additionally, Line 17 evaluates `if quiet for Q_e windows then` for every destination $d$ inside the loop without conditioning on $\text{state}_d \in \{\text{CONFIRMED}, \text{MITIGATING}\}$; under a literal reading, a benign destination in `NORMAL` would transition to `RECOVERING` after $Q_1 = 3$ quiet windows, again halting baseline updates at Line 7.
   - **Undefined Quiet-Window Predicate and Episode Counter Lifecycle ($Q_e$):** Neither Section IV-A nor Algorithm 1 formally specifies the mathematical predicate for a "quiet window" on Line 17 (e.g., whether it requires $s_d(t) < \eta$ or $B_d(t) = \emptyset$), when the episode counter $e$ in the exponential hold-down formula $Q_e = Q \cdot 2^{\min(e-1, 3)}$ increments, or under what prolonged benign condition $e$ resets to $1$.

2. **Discrepancies Between Equation (7), the Prose, and Algorithm 1 in Source Attribution and Cold-Start / Evasion Edge Cases:**
   Several consistency and operational boundary conditions in the source attribution logic (Section IV-B and Algorithm 1) represent potential concerns:
   - **Omission of Multi-Window Persistence and Repeat-Offender Terms in Equation (7):** Equation (7) defines the block set $B_d(t)$ purely as a single-window predicate at time $t$:
     $$B_d(t) = \left\{ s : \lambda_{s,d} \ge \lambda_{\text{floor}} \land \lambda_{s,d} \ge \beta \mu_{s,d} \land \left( r_{s,d} \ge \eta_s \lor (\lambda_{s,d} \ge \lambda_{\text{rate}} \land \text{corr}(s)) \right) \right\}.$$
     However, the prose at the end of Section IV-B states that "a source enters $B_d$ only after qualifying in two consecutive windows (repeat offenders at once)." Defining Eq. (7) as an instantaneous candidate set $\tilde{B}_d(t)$ and writing the persistence-filtered block set as $B_d(t) = \big(\tilde{B}_d(t) \cap \tilde{B}_d(t-1)\big) \cup \big(\tilde{B}_d(t) \cap R_d(t)\big)$ would align the formal equation with the prose and Line 14 of Algorithm 1. Similarly, in Section IV-A and Eq. (6), $R_d(t)$ is defined in prose as sources blocked at $d$ within the last $M = 300\text{ s}$ that are "sending again," without specifying whether "sending again" means any packet ($\lambda_{s,d}(t) > 0$), exceeding the rate floor ($\lambda_{s,d}(t) \ge \lambda_{\text{floor}}$), or satisfying $\tilde{B}_d(t)$—a distinction that matters because $R_d(t) \neq \emptyset$ allows a single window with $s_d(t) \ge \eta$ to trigger confirmation in Eq. (6).
   - **Gating Attribution on $s_d(t) \ge \eta$ in Algorithm 1:** Line 13 of Algorithm 1 gates source attribution and rule installation on `if state_d \in {CONFIRMED, MITIGATING} and s_d >= \eta then`. During low-rate or pulsing attacks—or once initial drop rules begin suppressing traffic—$s_d(t)$ can dip below $\eta$ for one or two windows (fewer than $Q_e$ quiet windows, so the episode remains active). Requiring $s_d(t) \ge \eta$ on Line 13 prevents two-window persistence from completing if a source's second qualifying window coincides with $s_d(t) < \eta$.
   - **Cold-Start Baseline ($\mu_{s,d} = 0$) for Newly Arriving Legitimate High-Rate Flows and Slow-Ramp Evasion:** In Section IV-B, $\text{corr}(s)$ holds "if $s$ was never seen sending to $d$ in normal windows." If a legitimate client initiates a new bulk transfer or video stream to $d$ for the first time while $d$ is already under a confirmed attack, its pre-attack baseline is $\mu_{s,d} = 0$ (trivially satisfying $\lambda_{s,d} \ge \beta \mu_{s,d}$) and $\text{corr}(s)$ is automatically satisfied via the "never seen in normal windows" clause. Consequently, any newly arriving legitimate source sending at $\ge \lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\text{ pps}$ (such as the $2\text{ Mbit/s}$ bulk TCP transfers in Section V-B, which average $\approx 166\text{ pps}$ at a 1500-byte MTU) for two consecutive windows would be blocked, potentially violating the zero-collateral constraint $|B_v \cap L| = 0$. Conversely, because $\lambda_{s,d} \ge \beta \mu_{s,d}$ is a mandatory conjunction in Eq. (7) and $\mu_{s,d} \leftarrow \max(\lambda_{s,d}, 0.97\mu_{s,d})$ updates during normal windows ($s_d(t) < \eta$) and decays slowly ($\approx 45\text{ s}$ half-life), a small number of attackers that ramp up their traffic while the aggregate score remains $s_d(t) < \eta$ could inflate $\mu_{s,d}$ above $\frac{1}{\beta}$ of their flood rate and evade attribution even when $r_{s,d} \ge \eta_s$.

3. **Interaction Between Ingress Drop Rules, Counter Deltas, Rule Timeouts, and Anti-Spoofing Port Quarantine:**
   In Section IV-C and Algorithm 1, mitigation installs priority-200 per-source drop rules with `hard_timeout = 300 s` at the attacker's ingress switch (Line 15) and reactive port-quarantine rules `(in_port=p, ipv4_dst=d, drop)` when a host port emits $\ge \kappa = 20$ spoofed packet-ins towards $d$ in one window (Line 21). Several operational interactions warrant clarification for reproducibility:
   - **Drop-Rule Counters vs. Quiet Windows:** Once ingress drop rules are active, traffic from blocked sources no longer matches standard forwarding entries along the path. Section IV does not state whether the per-window counter deltas $x_f(t)$ in Line 2 of Algorithm 1 (and thus $\lambda_{s,d}(t)$, $\phi_d(t)$, and $s_d(t)$) include packet counters from installed drop rules or only forwarding rules. If drop-rule counters are excluded from $\phi_d(t)$, blocking the attackers will immediately cause the observed forwarding traffic towards $d$ and the window score $s_d(t)$ to drop to normal levels in the next window, which under Line 17 (`if quiet for Q_e windows then remove the rules of d`) would prematurely remove the drop rules after $Q_1 = 3$ windows ($6\text{ s}$) while the flood is still actively hitting the ingress switches; conversely, if drop-rule counters are included, blocked attackers would appear to continue delivering traffic to $d$ during the quiet-window hold-down check.

### [Dataset Preparation, Testbed Methodology, and Baseline Calibration] (Pages: 3-4)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Victim Access Link Capacity Mismatch Between Calibration and Test Sessions (Sections IV-D and V-B):**
   In Section V-B (page 4), the testbed configuration specifies that *"The victim h200 sits behind a 25 Mbit/s access link in the test sessions (50 Mbit/s in calibration)."* Simultaneously, steady legitimate traffic delivers *"About 17 Mbit/s of benign load"* to the victim (including 10 bulk TCP transfers at 2 Mbit/s each), and flash crowds make all 95 web clients (60% targeting the victim) fetch a 100 kB page six times in 20 s (adding $\approx 95 \times 0.60 \times 6 \times 800\text{ kbit} / 20\text{ s} \approx 13.7\text{ Mbit/s}$ towards the victim, pushing total benign flash-crowd demand above $30\text{ Mbit/s}$). Because feature standardization statistics in $\psi(\cdot)$, the logistic regression weights ($w, b$ and $u, c$), and the $(1 - \varepsilon)$ decision thresholds ($\eta$, $\eta_s$, and all baseline thresholds) are fitted exclusively on the 50 Mbit/s calibration session (Section IV-D), halving the bottleneck capacity to 25 Mbit/s in the test sessions introduces a potential distributional shift:
   * Under a 50 Mbit/s link, 17 Mbit/s of base load plus 13.7 Mbit/s flash-crowd bursts pass without saturating the access link; under a 25 Mbit/s link, the same benign flash crowds exceed link capacity, inducing queuing delay, packet drops, TCP congestion window throttling, and shifts in bandwidth- and congestion-dependent Level-2 features (such as bit rate, packet rate $\Lambda_d$, response ratio $\rho_d$, and log rate change) relative to the calibration distribution.
   * Rate- and congestion-sensitive baselines (such as $\text{pps per destination } \Lambda_d(t) > \eta_\Lambda$) calibrated under a 50 Mbit/s ceiling may experience systematically different tail behavior when operating under a 25 Mbit/s bottleneck. Furthermore, Section IV-D does not specify the total number of benign calibration windows or whether the $(1 - \varepsilon)$ benign window quantile ($\varepsilon = 0.3\%$) is pooled across all $\approx 100$ active destinations (97 clients receiving light reply traffic and 3 servers) or stratified by destination role; pooling across clients and servers would concentrate the top $0.3\%$ tail windows on the heavily loaded victim server. The authors should explain the rationale for changing $C_v$ between calibration and testing (and clarify whether threshold calibration is pooled across all destinations), or verify that calibrating on a 25 Mbit/s link does not materially alter the false-positive rates and relative baseline rankings.

2. **Flow-Level Dataset Deduplication, Grouping Variable, and Benign Sample Count (Section V-A):**
   In Section V-A (page 3), the authors map CIC-DDoS2019 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 to the seven Level-1 features, *"remove all rows whose feature vectors are identical but whose labels differ,"* and state that *"After cleaning, CIC-DDoS2019 has 1 831 822 flows with only 16 733 benign (7 302 benign UDP)... Splits are group-disjoint; each model is trained on 20 000 flows per class per dataset and tested on natural-ratio holdouts of 60 000 flows per dataset."* Several methodological details needed to assess data leakage and reproducibility are missing:
   * **Benign sample count vs. training and holdout sizes in CIC-DDoS2019:** If only 16,733 benign flows remain in CIC-DDoS2019 after cleaning, it is unclear how 20,000 benign training flows *plus* the benign portion of the 60,000-flow natural-ratio test holdout were drawn in a group-disjoint manner without replacement. Please clarify whether benign flows in CIC-DDoS2019 were oversampled with replacement after splitting, or whether the 20,000-per-class figure applies prior to deduplication.
   * **Definition of the grouping variable:** The text does not specify what variable defines a "group" for the group-disjoint split (e.g., `(source IP, destination IP)` pair, capture timestamp/time block, or attack sub-type), which is important because IP addresses and timestamps are not part of the 7-feature vector $z_f$ and flows from the same flood session share nearly identical rates and packet sizes.
   * **Deduplication scope and class-prior shift:** When projecting 80+ CICFlowMeter features onto 7 coarse counter features (protocol, duration, packets, bytes, packet rate, byte rate, mean packet size), thousands of short single-packet or two-packet flows (e.g., UDP queries, pings, or SYN packets) collapse onto identical 7-tuple feature vectors. It is unclear whether identical feature vectors *within* the same class were deduplicated prior to splitting, and whether cross-label deduplication was performed across the entire dataset before train/test splitting (which would use test-set labels to filter the holdout distribution). In addition, training $g_\theta(z_f)$ on a 1:1 class-balanced subsample shifts the uncalibrated posterior probabilities $p_f = g_\theta(z_f)$ relative to the natural base rate, affecting the fixed $p_f \ge 0.5$ threshold used in Level-2 "flagged-flow fraction" and "flagged-packet share" features as well as $\text{corr}(s)$ in Level 3. Clarifying whether $p_f$ is recalibrated or kept at the 1:1 prior would improve reproducibility.

   * **Asymmetric confirmation logic in latency comparisons:** Section V-C states that *"All baselines use the same features, the same 2-of-3 confirmation and thresholds set on calibration data at the same false-positive budget."* While Table II reports the proposed detector both with and without the fast path (`Proposed (2-of-3)` vs. `Proposed + fast path`), the per-attack latency comparison in Table III evaluates the proposed detector (`Prop.`) *with* the single-window fast path ($s_d(t) \ge \eta_f = 0.97$) and repeat-offender trigger ($s_d(t) \ge \eta \wedge R_d(t) \neq \emptyset$) from Eq.    * **Source-IP entropy normalization and alarm directionality:** Section V-C defines baseline (ii) as *"source-IP entropy of the packet distribution over sources at $d$ (bits),"* whereas Level 2 (Section III-B, page 2) uses *normalised* source entropy $H_d = -\sum_s q_s \log q_s / \log n_d \in [0, 1]$. While Section V-C explicitly states that destination-IP entropy (baseline iii) is normalized and alarms *"when it drops,"* it does not specify why unnormalized entropy in bits ($-\sum_s q_s \log_2 q_s$, which scales with $\log_2 n_d$) was chosen for baseline (ii) instead of $H_d$, nor whether baseline (ii) triggers an alarm on an upper tail (entropy *increase*, when 40 distributed attackers join a destination normally served by fewer concurrent sources in a 2 s window), a lower tail (entropy *decrease*, when a single source dominates), or a two-sided threshold. ### [Empirical Results, Statistical Analysis, Ablations, and Claims Verification] (Pages: 4-7)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Test-Schedule Data Leakage and Adaptive Overfitting in Source Attribution Design (Sections VI-C, VI-D, VI-E, VII, and Table V)**
   - **Potential Methodological Concern:** A central claim in Section VI-C, Table IV, and Section VII (as well as the Abstract) is that the mitigation system blocks all 40 attackers while achieving **zero collateral damage** (*"no legitimate host was blocked,"* $|B_v \cap L| = 0$). However, Section VI-D (Table V) and Section VI-E disclose that the final source attribution logic was iteratively engineered across four successive runs (`v1` $\to$ `v2` $\to$ `v3` $\to$ `final`) evaluated directly on the **held-out test schedule (`seed 2`)**. Specifically, after observing 9, 6, and 5 blocked legitimate hosts in live mitigation runs on `seed 2`, the authors sequentially introduced the pre-attack baseline multiplier ($\beta \mu_{s,d}$), the counter-delta re-installation fix, new-source corroboration, and the 2-window persistence requirement, validating each change by replaying a detection-only trace from the same `seed 2` test schedule before executing the next live run. Using the held-out evaluation schedule to diagnose false positives and engineer structural decision rules until collateral blocks reach $0$ constitutes adaptive overfitting (data snooping) on the test set. Furthermore, in Table V, run `v1` was *"stopped after 5 of 14 segments"* (footnote $\S$), meaning its 9 blocked legitimate hosts occurred over roughly one-third of the schedule and are not evaluated over the same time horizon as the full 14-segment runs (`v2`–`final`). While the transparent disclosure in Section VI-E (*"a fresh seed would be a cleaner confirmation"*) is commendable, Section VI-C, Table IV, and Section VII present the $0$-collateral result as an unqualified held-out test outcome rather than an in-sample post-refinement fit.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Either evaluate the frozen `final` controller configuration on at least one genuinely unseen random seed (`seed 3`, with newly randomized client traffic timings, bitrates, attacker subsets, and attack orderings) to substantiate out-of-sample zero-collateral performance, or explicitly qualify the zero-collateral claim in Section VI-C, Section VII, and the Abstract to reflect that the attribution filters were iteratively tuned on the `seed 2` test schedule. In Table V, clarify whether all 40 attackers and 9 legitimate hosts in `v1` were already blocked within the first 5 segments, and consider reporting the 5-segment collateral counts for `v2`–`final` alongside their 14-segment totals for an apples-to-apples ablation comparison.

2. **Confounding of Continuous Test Schedules and Paired Per-Attack Mitigation Results by Sequential State Carryover and Unmitigated Control-Plane Collapse (Sections V-B, VI-C, Table IV, and Fig. 5)**
   - **Potential Concern (Causal Attribution Across Sequential Episodes):** The test schedule (Section V-B) and the paired evaluation between the unmitigated baseline (`test_det`, `test_det2`) and the mitigated run (`test_mit`) execute all nine attack episodes sequentially within a single continuous session separated by $40\text{ s}$ benign gaps (*"attacks last 30–105 s with 40 s gaps"*). Because several network and controller recovery timescales exceed $40\text{ s}$, running all episodes back-to-back without resetting switch and controller state introduces strong confounding effects across consecutive episodes:
     1. *Unrecovered control plane in unmitigated runs and apparent full recovery without active rule installation in Low-Rate UDP:* As reported in Section VI-C (page 5), in the unmitigated runs, *"from the ICMP flood onward legitimate service collapses and does not recover between attacks"* because the switch `packet-in` queues (rate-limited to $400\text{ pps}$ per switch) remain saturated. Consequently, during the six attack episodes following the third attack (ICMP)—namely `low-rate`, `pulsing`, `spoofed`, `mixed`, `protocol switching`, and `re-attack`—the $40\text{ s}$ inter-attack gaps do not return the network to a clean benign steady state ($H_0$), and in detection-only runs, the destination-centric window features $\phi_d(t)$ (such as active legitimate flows, byte response ratio $\rho_d$, and source entropy $H_d$) are evaluated over an already-collapsed background traffic distribution. In Table IV, the fourth attack episode (**Low-rate**, where attackers send $25\text{--}45\text{ pps}$) reports `0/40` new rules installed (`1st rule: –`, and unlike `Proto. switch` and `Re-attack`, it is not marked with footnote $\ddagger$), yet legitimate HTTP success is reported as rising from $0.06 \to 1.00$, UDP query success from $0.00 \to 1.00$, and Ping delivery from $0.34 \to 0.99$. This creates two unaddressed anomalies:
        - First, regarding why $0/40$ rules were installed: although Low-rate UDP is detected at the destination level (latency $5.1\text{ s}$ in Table III) and each attacker's rate ($25\text{--}45\text{ pps}$) exceeds the attribution floor $\lambda_{\text{floor}} = 15\text{ pps}$ (Eq. 7), individual attackers fall well below the rate-corroboration threshold $\lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\text{ pps}$ and evidently do not exceed the logistic source score threshold $\eta_s$. Thus, low-rate floods are **detected but not actively attributed/mitigated** ($0/40$ rules installed), making them a second unmitigated vector alongside ICMP floods.
        - Second, regarding why HTTP success jumps from $0.06 \to 1.00$ with $0$ new rules installed: the $0.06$ HTTP success rate during the unmitigated Low-rate episode is largely a carryover artifact of persistent control-plane collapse from earlier floods rather than congestion caused by the Low-rate traffic itself ($40 \times 35\text{ pps} \approx 1.4\text{ kpps}$, which does not saturate the $25\text{ Mbit/s}$ link or the $400\text{ pps/switch}$ `packet-in` limit). Conversely, in `test_mit`, the text does not explain how the network achieved $1.00$ HTTP success during Low-rate despite the immediately preceding ICMP flood only blocking $1/40$ attackers (e.g., whether the $38/40$ and $36/40$ all-protocol drop rules from the earlier UDP and TCP SYN episodes—which have a $300\text{ s}$ hard timeout—remained active across the ICMP and Low-rate episodes). Crediting the mitigation system with a $0.06 \to 1.00$ recovery on Low-rate UDP and folding it into the headline $48\% \to 89\%$ average across nine attacks conflates preventing earlier control-plane collapse (or residual rules from earlier floods) with actively mitigating the low-rate attack.
     2. *Hold-down timer ($Q_e \Delta = 48\text{ s}$), repeat-offender memory ($M = 300\text{ s}$), and $300\text{ s}$ rule-timeout carryover in Protocol Switching and Re-attack:* Section IV-A specifies that a destination's drop rules are removed after $Q_e = Q \cdot 2^{\min(e-1, 3)}$ quiet windows ($Q = 3$, $\Delta = 2\text{ s}$), while repeat-offender memory $R_d(t)$ persists for $M = 300\text{ s}$ and Section IV-C sets a $300\text{ s}$ hard timeout on OpenFlow rules. Once the episode count reaches $e \ge 4$, the hold-down duration reaches $24 \text{ windows} \times 2\text{ s} = 48\text{ s}$, which exceeds the $40\text{ s}$ benign gap between scheduled attacks. As noted by footnote $\ddagger$ in Table IV, during **Protocol switching** (`0/24` new rules installed) and **Re-attack** (`4/30` new rules installed), most attackers were already blocked prior to episode onset by rules carried over from the preceding **Mixed** episode ($34/40$ rules), and any unblocked returning attackers immediately trigger repeat-offender confirmation ($R_d(t) \neq \emptyset$). Therefore, the Protocol-switching episode only confirms that pre-existing all-protocol `ipv4_src`/`ipv4_dst` rules from an earlier episode did not expire during the $40\text{ s}$ gap; it does not test whether the controller can dynamically detect, attribute, and block an unblocked attacker switching protocols mid-episode.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Clarify in Section V-B how the 40 attackers are sampled or reused across consecutive episodes within a session, state in Section VI-C, Section VI-E, and the Table IV caption that Low-rate attacks are detected but not attributed (`0/40` rules installed), and explain why `test_mit` achieves $1.00$ HTTP success during the Low-rate episode despite `1/40` rules in ICMP and `0/40` in Low-rate. To isolate cold-start detection latency and per-attack mitigation capability from schedule-ordering and carryover artifacts, either report paired runs where switch flow tables, `packet-in` queues, and controller state ($R_d(t)$ and $e$) are reset before each attack type (or extend inter-attack gaps beyond $\max(Q_e \Delta, 300\text{ s})$), or explicitly separate active per-episode mitigation from carryover effects when computing summary averages in Table IV.

3. **Unexplained Systems Divergence Between Live ($1/40$) and Offline Replay ($38/40$) Attribution on ICMP Floods (Section VI-C, Section VI-E, and Table IV)**
   - **Potential Concern (Incomplete Systems Diagnosis):** Section VI-E (p. 5) and Table IV (p. 6) report that during the live ICMP flood, the detector confirms the attack in $1.3\text{ s}$ and installs a first drop rule at $3.7\text{ s}$, but attributes only $1\text{ of } 40$ attackers live (resulting in no HTTP recovery: $0.73 \to 0.74$, and severe Ping degradation: $0.16 \to 0.22$), whereas *"the same rule attributed 38 of 40 in an offline replay."* The paper reports this discrepancy as a limitation but provides no technical diagnosis of *why* the identical attribution formula (Eq. 7) succeeds on the recorded detection trace (`test_det2`) yet fails in the live mitigation loop (`test_mit`). Given the system architecture (Sections IV and V-B)—where forwarding rules are installed per `(source, destination, protocol)`, switches rate-limit `packet-in` messages to $400\text{ pps}$, and Algorithm 1 waits up to $1.5\text{ s}$ for switch flow statistics—transitioning to a new protocol (ICMP) triggers a burst of `packet-in` messages that may delay flow-entry installation or `OFPFlowStatsReply` counter updates, preventing $\lambda_{s,d}(t)$ from meeting the 2-window persistence condition while $s_d(t) \ge \eta$ in Algorithm 1 (line 13). Alternatively, if $36\text{--}38$ attackers were still blocked at their ingress switches by all-protocol drop rules from the preceding UDP/SYN episodes at ICMP onset (or if those rules expired mid-episode), the live traffic reaching the switches during ICMP in `test_mit` would differ fundamentally from the unblocked traffic in the detection-only replay (`test_det2`).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Explain the exact root cause of the live ($1/40$) vs. offline ($38/40$) ICMP attribution gap in Section VI-E, specifying which condition in Eq. (7) (rate floor $\lambda_{\text{floor}}$, baseline $\beta\mu_{s,d}$, source score $r_{s,d}$, corroboration $\text{corr}(s)$, or 2-window persistence) failed for the 37 attackers in the live run (`test_mit`) compared to the offline replay (`test_det2`).

4. **Missing Architectural Ablation to Substantiate the Claim Regarding Level-1 Flow-ML Utility (Section V-C, Section VI-A, Section VI-B, Section VII, and Table II)**
   - **Potential Concern (Unverified Ablation Claim):** In Section VI-A (p. 4) and Section VII (p. 6), the paper makes a central architectural claim: *"The gains of the system come from Levels 2 and 3, not from the classifier"* and *"the flow classifier is not where the gains come from."* However, Section V-C and Table II only compare the full three-level system (`Proposed`, Levels 1 + 2 + 3, $\text{F1} = 0.78$) against `Flow-ML only` (Level 1 alone, $\max_f p_f$ at $d$, $\text{F1} = 0.54$) and single-feature statistical baselines (`pps`, `Source-IP entropy`, `Dest.-IP entropy`). 5. **Apparent Contradiction Between Table I Balanced Accuracy and McNemar Test Point Deltas in Section VI-A**
   - **Potential Concern (Statistical Clarity / Metric Mismatch):** In Section VI-A (p. 4), the text states: *"Table I compares all models trained on both datasets. HGB, Random Forest, Decision Tree and KNN are within 0.5 points; McNemar tests [26], [27] on the same 120 000 test flows find RF ($-0.42$ points, $p = 4 \times 10^{-18}$), KNN ($-0.26$, $p = 7 \times 10^{-8}$) and DT ($-0.23$, $p = 5 \times 10^{-6}$) slightly but significantly better than HGB."* Cross-checking this statement against Table I (p. 5) reveals an apparent contradiction:

     - The reason RF, KNN, and DT appear better by $0.23\text{--}0.42$ percentage points in the McNemar test is that standard McNemar tests evaluate discordant predictions on **unweighted overall accuracy** across the $120\,000$ pooled natural-ratio test flows ($60\,000$ per dataset, where CIC-DDoS2019 is $>99\%$ attack and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 is $\approx 70\%$ benign), rather than Balanced Accuracy. Additionally, because $\text{BA} = \frac{1}{2}(\text{Recall} + 1 - \text{FPR})$, noting briefly in Section VI-A that Naive Bayes achieves low FPR ($1.8\%$ and $0.6\%$) only because its attack recall is near zero ($20.6\%$ on '19 and $0.2\%$ on '18, vs. $98.7\%$ and $95.5\%$ for HGB) would make Table I easier to interpret.

6. **Survivorship Bias in Conditional Mean Detection Latencies Across Baselines (Section VI-B, Table II, and Table III)**
   - **Potential Concern (Quantitative Reporting):** In Table III (p. 5), the bottom row (`Mean`) reports mean detection latencies across the nine attack types (`3.2 s` for Proposed, `5.4 s` for DstEnt, `7.5 s` for pps, `6.9 s` for SrcEnt, and `7.7 s` for ML), which also match the pooled `Lat.` column in Table II. For the baselines that completely failed to detect certain attack episodes (`–: NOT DETECTED` in Table III, where `pps` misses both Low-rate UDP episodes, `SrcEnt` misses both Protocol-switching episodes, `ML` misses both TCP SYN flood episodes, and `DstEnt` misses one of the two Spoofed sessions marked with $\dagger$), the reported mean is simply the average over the *detected* attack rows/episodes, silently omitting missed episodes:
     - `pps`: $(1.7 + 7.9 + 1.3 + 8.9 + 2.6 + 23.7 + 6.2 + 7.7) / 8 = 60.0 / 8 = 7.50\text{ s}$.
     - `SrcEnt`: $(1.7 + 3.7 + 1.3 + 5.1 + 7.4 + 2.6 + 24.7 + 8.7) / 8 = 55.2 / 8 = 6.90\text{ s}$.
     - `ML`: $(5.7 + 23.9 + 5.1 + 8.1 + 4.8 + 6.6 + 5.3 + 2.4) / 8 = 61.9 / 8 = 7.7375 \approx 7.7\text{ s}$.
     - `DstEnt`: weighted over its $17$ detected episodes across the two sessions, $(2 \times (5.7 + 5.9 + 12.5 + 1.1 + 5.0 + 5.4 + 4.1 + 3.7) + 4.7) / 17 = 91.5 / 17 = 5.38 \approx 5.4\text{ s}$.
     Conditioning mean latency strictly on detected episodes introduces survivorship bias: when a baseline fails to detect a difficult attack entirely (which would otherwise incur a delay of at least the $30\text{--}105\text{ s}$ episode duration), its mean latency is not penalized, making baseline latencies appear closer to the proposed method (`3.2 s` across all $18/18$ episodes).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** State explicitly in the captions or footnotes of Tables II and III that mean detection latencies are computed *over detected episodes only* (conditional detection delay), and consider reporting either the mean over the common subset of attack episodes detected by all methods or a right-censored/penalized mean (setting missed episodes to the episode duration).

7. **Ambiguity Between Pre-Confirmation Window Metrics and Post-Confirmation Episode Metrics in Table II and Fig. 3 (Section VI-B and Section VI-E)**
   - **Potential Concern (Reproducibility & Metric Definition):** Across the $78\,141$ pooled destination windows ($77\,755$ benign, $386$ under attack), the reported window metrics for `Proposed` in Table II (`Rec. = 0.97`, `FPR = 0.26%`, `F1 = 0.78`) and Section VI-E (`Window precision (0.65)`) are arithmetically consistent with an underlying confusion matrix of $\text{TP} = 374$ ($\text{Rec.} = 374/386 = 0.9689 \approx 0.97$), $\text{FN} = 12$, and $\text{FP} = 202$ ($\text{FPR} = 202/77\,755 = 0.2598\% \approx 0.26\%$), yielding $\text{Precision} = 374/(374+202) = 0.6493 \approx 0.65$ and $\text{F1} = 2 \times 374 / (2 \times 374 + 202 + 12) = 0.7775 \approx 0.78$. However, two ambiguities arise in how these numbers are presented:
     1. In Table II, `Proposed (2-of-3)` and `Proposed + fast path` have identical `Rec.` ($0.97$), `FPR` ($0.26\%$), and `F1` ($0.78$), yet their mean detection latencies (`4.5 s` vs. `3.2 s`) and false-alarm episode rates (`40 FA/h` vs. `45 FA/h`) differ. It is unclear whether `Rec.`, `FPR`, and `F1` are evaluated on the raw single-window threshold $\mathbb{1}\{s_d(t) \ge \eta\}$ (or 2-of-3 without the fast path) while `Det.`, `Lat.`, and `FA/h` are evaluated on the state-machine confirmation rule $\text{confirm}_d(t) = 1$ (Eq. 6), or whether the additional fast-path confirmations simply do not change `Rec.`, `FPR`, and `F1` after two-decimal rounding.
     2. In Section VI-B (pp. 4–5), the text states *"Fig. 3 shows the window-level comparison for `test_det`. The proposed detector has the highest recall (0.97) and F1 (0.78) at a 0.26% window false-positive rate..."* Here, the prose cites the *pooled two-session* numbers from Table II ($0.26\%$ FPR) while referring to Fig. 3, whose caption and x-axis labels are for the single session `test_det` (`FPR 0.3%` and `0.4%`).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Specify in Section VI-B and the Table II footnote whether `Rec.`, `FPR`, and `F1` are computed on the pre-confirmation window score $s_d(t) \ge \eta$ or on the post-confirmation state $\text{confirm}_d(t) = 1$, and clearly distinguish between the single-session labels in Fig. 3 (`test_det`) and the pooled two-session metrics in Table II.
