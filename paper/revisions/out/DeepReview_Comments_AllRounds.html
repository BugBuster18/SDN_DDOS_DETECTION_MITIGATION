## Summary
This paper investigates machine-learning-based DDoS detection and source-scoped mitigation in OpenFlow-based Software-Defined Networks (SDN). Motivated by the observation that flow-level classifiers trained on single public datasets suffer from severe domain shift and fail in live deployments, the authors formulate SDN DDoS defense as a quickest-change-detection problem under a false-alarm budget coupled with a goodput-maximizing, collateral-free block-set selection problem. They propose and evaluate a three-level Ryu controller architecture—combining a cross-dataset flow-level gradient-boosting model (Level 1), destination-centric OpenFlow counter-delta window features (Level 2), and a calibrated logistic state machine with baseline-aware, persistence-filtered source attribution and reactive port quarantine (Level 3)—on a 200-host, 25-switch Mininet/Open vSwitch testbed across nine attack scenarios.

## Strengths
* **Practical Problem Framing and Cross-Dataset Empirical Insights:** The paper directly confronts the operational gap between offline per-flow ML classification and live SDN defense, demonstrating empirically that single-dataset flow classifiers fail catastrophically across datasets (scoring below chance at 0.42–0.47 balanced accuracy) and that defense requires destination-centric temporal aggregation coupled with source-scoped mitigation.
* **Thoughtful Engineering of Destination Telemetry and Source Attribution:** The system incorporates practical systems mechanisms to reduce collateral damage when benign high-rate senders and attackers share edge switches, including re-installation-aware OpenFlow counter-delta corrections, pre-attack per-source traffic baselines ($\lambda_{s,d} \ge \beta \mu_{s,d}$), two-window persistence filtering, protocol-agnostic ingress drop rules, and reactive port quarantine.
* **Comprehensive Testbed Evaluation and Transparent Reporting of Limitations:** The authors evaluate end-to-end application goodput (HTTP, DNS, Ping, bulk TCP) across a 200-host topology and nine diverse attack types against four calibrated baselines, while candidly reporting negative results such as live ICMP attribution failure, high network-wide false-alarm episode rates due to the base-rate fallacy, and test-schedule tuning limitations.

## Weaknesses
* **Missing Architectural Ablation Without the Level-1 Flow Classifier:** Although the authors conclude in Sections VI-A and VII that the system's gains come from Levels 2 and 3 rather than the flow classifier, the evaluation only compares the full pipeline (Levels 1+2+3) against Level-1-only (`Flow-ML only`) and single-feature rules; the authors might consider adding an ablation baseline using only Levels 2 and 3 (the 15 OpenFlow counter window features and 8 source-pair features without Level-1 probabilities $p_f$) to verify whether the Level-1 model provides any net improvement in detection F1 or source attribution.
* **Disconnect Between Formal Mathematical Optimization and the Implemented Heuristics:** The mathematical formulations in Section III-D–E (Equations 2–4) are not rigorously connected to the implemented controller in Section IV: single-window quantile calibration ($\varepsilon = 0.3\%$) does not formally bound the multi-trigger disjunctive confirmation rule in Eq. (6), the latency lower bound $D \ge (k-1+U)\Delta$ in Eq. (4) fails to hold when single-window fast-path or repeat-offender triggers fire (as seen in the 1.2–1.3 s latencies in Table III), Eq. (3) exhibits a unit mismatch between packet rates $\lambda_{s,d}$ and bit-rate capacity $C_v$ while leaving $G_{s,v}$ uncoupled from $B_v$, and the flow-table budget $|B_v| \le R_{\max}$ is never enforced in Eq. (7) or Algorithm 1. Furthermore, because the problem is framed as quickest change detection and constrained SDN resource allocation, the authors might consider clarifying how their heuristic relates to established sequential change-point detection and constrained SDN mitigation frameworks (Reference Paper: Procedures for Reacting to a Change in Distribution, Gary Lorden, 1971; Reference Paper: Bohatei: Flexible and Elastic DDoS Defense, Seyed K. Fayaz et al., 2015).
* **Test-Schedule Data Snooping in Source Attribution Refinement:** As noted in Sections VI-D and VI-E, the source attribution rules (`v1` through `final` in Table V) were iteratively engineered while observing collateral blocks directly on the held-out `seed 2` test schedule, which constitutes adaptive data snooping (Reference Paper: Dos and Don'ts of Machine Learning in Computer Security, Daniel Arp et al., 2022). Please consider validating the frozen controller configuration on a fresh, unseen random seed (`seed 3`) or qualifying the headline claim of zero collateral blocking accordingly.
* **Confounding State Carryover Across Sequential Test Episodes and Unexplained Live ICMP Failure:** Running all nine attacks sequentially with 40 s gaps causes unmitigated control-plane collapse to persist from the third attack (ICMP) onward and allows 300 s drop rules and 48 s hold-down timers ($Q_e\Delta$) to carry over across episodes in the mitigated run, which confounds per-attack metrics (e.g., `Low-rate` UDP shows a $0.06 \to 1.00$ HTTP recovery despite installing $0/40$ rules). Additionally, please consider diagnosing the specific root cause of why live ICMP attribution blocks only $1/40$ attackers compared to $38/40$ in offline replay.
* **Positioning Relative to Multi-Stage SDN Anomaly Detection and SAVI Prior Art:** While the synthesis of components is practical, the individual building blocks—combining window-level entropy/counter features with ML and per-source mitigation, as well as port-level source address validation—build closely on established paradigms; the authors might consider expanding their discussion of prior multi-stage SDN detectors and discussing how single-IP-per-port bindings ($\beta(\text{sw}, p)$) and `(src, dst)` drop rules behave in shared-port or NAT environments (Reference Paper: Combining OpenFlow and sFlow for an Effective and Scalable Anomaly Detection and Mitigation Mechanism on SDN Environments, Kostas Giotis et al., 2014; Reference Paper: Long Short-Term Memory and Fuzzy Logic for Anomaly Detection and Mitigation in Software-Defined Network Environment, Matheus P. Novaes et al., 2020; Reference Paper: Source Address Validation Improvement (SAVI) Framework, Jianping Wu et al., 2013).

### [Problem Framing, Related Work, and Mathematical Formulation] (Pages: 1-2)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Uncoupled Objective Function, Dimensional Mismatch, and Unenforced Budget in the Mitigation Optimization Problem (Section III-D, Equation 3):**
   There are three potential mathematical and modeling concerns in how the block-set selection problem is formulated in Equation (3):
   $$\max_{B_v} \sum_{s \in L} G_{s,v} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{a \in A \setminus B_v} \lambda_{a,v} + \sum_{s \in L} \lambda_{s,v} \le C_v, \quad |B_v \cap L| = 0, \quad |B_v| \le R_{\max}.$$
   - *Uncoupled Objective and Hard Link-Capacity Constraint:* In the objective $\max_{B_v} \sum_{s \in L} G_{s,v}$, legitimate goodput $G_{s,v}$ is never formally defined as a function of the decision variable $B_v$ (or of the residual bottleneck load $\sum_{a \in A \setminus B_v} \lambda_{a,v}$). Furthermore, under the strict zero-collateral constraint $|B_v \cap L| = 0$ and the hard link-capacity inequality on offered rates $\sum_{a \in A \setminus B_v} \lambda_{a,v} + \sum_{s \in L} \lambda_{s,v} \le C_v$, any feasible block set $B_v$ leaves all legitimate traffic unblocked and within link capacity $C_v$. Consequently, $G_{s,v}$ is constant across all feasible $B_v$, reducing Eq. (3) to a pure feasibility problem rather than a maximization problem. Conversely, treating $C_v$ as a hard constraint on offered rates renders the problem strictly infeasible whenever $|A| > R_{\max}$ (or when attackers remain unattributed) such that blocking at most $R_{\max}$ attackers cannot bring the residual offered rate below $C_v - \sum_{s \in L} \lambda_{s,v}$. In reality, an overloaded link drops excess packets and degrades goodput (or saturates the control channel) rather than making a choice of $B_v$ mathematically invalid. Expressing $G_{s,v}(B_v)$ explicitly as the congestion-degraded goodput on a bottleneck link of capacity $C_v$ (e.g., under proportional link sharing when total offered load exceeds $C_v$, or by minimizing residual unblocked attack load) and dropping the hard capacity inequality on offered rates would make the optimization problem well-posed.
   - *Dimensional / Unit Mismatch Between $\lambda_{s,d}$ and $C_v$:* In Section III-B (Eq. 1), $\lambda_{s,d}(t)$ is defined from cumulative packet counters $P_f(t)$ as a *packet rate* in packets per second (consistent with $\lambda_{\text{floor}} = 15\text{ pps}$ and $\lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\text{ pps}$ in Section IV-B), and Section III-C explicitly notes that volumetric floods have $\sum_{a \in A} \lambda_{a,v}$ far above "$C_v$ divided by packet size." In Eq. (3), however, packet rates $\lambda_{a,v}$ and $\lambda_{s,v}$ (in pps) are summed and compared directly against the link capacity $C_v$ (measured in Mbit/s in Section V-B) without multiplying by packet size or converting to bit rates.
   - *Disconnect Between Flow-Table Budget $R_{\max}$ and the Plug-In Solver:* The Abstract, Section I, and Eq. (3) highlight the flow-table budget constraint $|B_v| \le R_{\max}$ as a core component of the formal problem statement, stating that "$B_v$ is estimated by the attribution rule of Section IV." However, neither the plug-in attribution rule in Section IV-B (Eq. 7) nor the mitigation procedure in Section IV-C (Algorithm 1, Lines 14–15) enforces a cardinality cap $R_{\max}$ or specifies a ranking criterion (e.g., selecting the top-$R_{\max}$ qualifying sources in $B_d(t)$ by descending packet rate $\lambda_{s,d}(t)$ or source score $r_{s,d}$) when $|B_d(t)| > R_{\max}$. Similarly, Eq. (3) models mitigation solely as selecting a host subset $B_v \subset H$, whereas spoofed floods are mitigated via switch-port quarantine rules (`in_port=p, ipv4_dst=d`) in Section IV-C. Incorporating a truncation/ranking step or clarifying how $R_{\max}$ and port quarantine relate to the implemented plug-in solver would resolve this discrepancy.

2. **Quickest-Change-Detection Parameterization, Calibration Alignment, and Time Indexing (Section III-D, Equation 2 and Section IV-D):**
   Equation (2) poses detection as minimizing expected detection delay over both model parameters and confirmation hyperparameters:
   $$\min_{w,b,\eta,k,n,\eta_f} \mathbb{E}[D], \quad D = \min\{t \ge \tau_a : \mathrm{confirm}_v(t) = 1\} - \tau_a \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \Pr(\mathrm{confirm}_d(t) = 1 \mid H_0) \le \varepsilon.$$
   Several aspects of this formulation present potential concerns regarding mathematical well-posedness and alignment with the calibration procedure in Section IV-D:
   - *Degeneracy Over Confirmation Hyperparameters $(k, n, \eta_f)$ and Disconnect from Parameter Fitting:* If $(\eta, \eta_f, k, n)$ are treated as free minimization variables in Eq. (2) subject only to the marginal constraint $\Pr(\mathrm{confirm}_d(t) = 1 \mid H_0) \le \varepsilon$, the optimal solution trivially collapses to $k = 1, n = 1$ (or $\eta_f = \eta$), making the $k$-of-$n$ state machine redundant because a 1-of-1 threshold calibrated to the $(1 - \varepsilon)$ quantile of $s_d(t)$ under $H_0$ already satisfies the constraint with strictly smaller delay $D$. In practice (Sections IV-A and IV-D), the controller does not jointly optimize $(w, b, \eta, k, n, \eta_f)$ to minimize $\mathbb{E}[D]$: $(k, n) = (2, 3)$ and $\eta_f = 0.97$ are fixed architectural constants, and $(w, b)$ are fitted via single-window class-balanced logistic regression.
   - *Single-Window Quantile Calibration vs. Confirmation-Level False-Alarm Constraint:* In Eq. (2) (and Section I, "under a per-window false-alarm budget"), the constraint is written on the multi-window confirmation event $\Pr(\mathrm{confirm}_d(t) = 1 \mid H_0) \le \varepsilon$ and states that "the threshold $\eta$ is set on calibration data to meet $\varepsilon$." However, in Section IV-D, $\eta$ is calibrated solely as the $(1 - \varepsilon)$ quantile of single-window leave-one-segment-out probabilities $s_d(t)$ on benign windows ($\Pr(s_d(t) \ge \eta \mid H_0) \approx \varepsilon = 0.3\%$), while $\eta_f = 0.97$ is fixed. Because Equation (6) defines confirmation as the disjunction of a 2-of-3 sequential rule over $\eta$, the fast path $s_d(t) \ge \eta_f$, and the repeat-offender condition $(s_d(t) \ge \eta \wedge R_d(t) \neq \emptyset)$—and because consecutive $2\text{ s}$ window scores are temporally autocorrelated—setting the marginal single-window exceedance rate to $\varepsilon$ does not make the composite confirmation false-alarm probability $\Pr(\mathrm{confirm}_d(t) = 1 \mid H_0)$ equal to $\varepsilon$. In addition, Eq. (2) does not specify the probability distribution over which $\mathbb{E}[D]$ is taken (e.g., averaging over a uniform polling offset $U \in (0, 1]$ or taking a worst-case supremum over $\tau_a$ as in Lorden [18]), and Section IV-D does not define what constitutes a "segment" in the "leave-one-segment-out probabilities on benign windows" procedure or how attack segments are partitioned during class-balanced logistic regression fitting to prevent temporal leakage.
   - *Discrete Window Index vs. Continuous Time Units:* Section III-B defines $t = 1, 2, \dots$ as a dimensionless discrete window index for windows of length $\Delta$, whereas $\tau_a$ in Section III-A and latency $D \ge (k - 1 + U)\Delta$ in Section III-E (Eq. 4) are measured in continuous time (seconds). Writing $D = \min\{t \ge \tau_a : \mathrm{confirm}_v(t) = 1\} - \tau_a$ in Eq. (2) directly subtracts a continuous timestamp $\tau_a$ from a discrete window index $t$. Writing the stopping condition in terms of window completion times $t\Delta \ge \tau_a$ (i.e., $D = \min\{t\Delta \ge \tau_a : \mathrm{confirm}_v(t) = 1\} - \tau_a$) would make the units of $D$ consistent between Eqs. (2) and (4).

3. **Unformalized Counter-Delta Adjustments for Re-Installed Flow Entries and Switch Indexing (Section III-B, Equation 1):**
   In Section III-B, Eq. (1) defines the per-window packet delta strictly as the plain difference:
   $$x_f(t) = \max_{\mathrm{sw} \in \mathrm{path}} \big[ P_f(t) - P_f(t-1) \big].$$
   The prose immediately following Eq. (1) states two critical exceptions: *"When an entry is re-installed, only the share of its counter that falls inside the window is counted; when an entry is re-added with continuous counters, the plain difference is used."* Because Section VI-D (Table V) credits this counter-delta correction with eliminating $6\times$ one-window rate spikes (e.g., a $104\text{ pps}$ stream measured at $629\text{ pps}$) and preventing collateral blocking of legitimate hosts, two aspects of its specification warrant clarification:
   - *Missing Mathematical Definition of Re-installation Adjustment:* First, the cumulative counter $P_f(t)$ depends on the switch $\mathrm{sw}$, which is omitted from the notation $P_{f,\mathrm{sw}}(t)$. Second, Eq. (1) does not reflect the re-installation adjustment mathematically, nor is the formula for *"the share of its counter that falls inside the window"* defined anywhere in the paper (for instance, whether $P_{f,\mathrm{sw}}(t)$ is scaled by $\min(1, \Delta / \mathrm{duration}_{f,\mathrm{sw}}(t))$ when $P_{f,\mathrm{sw}}(t-1)$ belongs to an expired rule, or how the controller distinguishes a reset re-installed entry from a continuous-counter re-added entry using OpenFlow flow statistics). Providing the explicit piecewise formula for $x_f(t)$ is necessary to make this key mechanism reproducible.
   - *Asynchronous Polling Across Switches:* Taking $\max_{\mathrm{sw} \in \mathrm{path}} [P_f(t) - P_f(t-1)]$ assumes that all switches along the path represent active transit of flow $f$ polled over identical intervals. Because the controller polls 25 switches over a window of up to $1.5\text{ s}$ (Algorithm 1, Line 1), taking the maximum across all switches on a path can systematically select whichever switch experienced the longest inter-poll interval due to polling jitter.

4. **Scope and Assumptions of the Detection Latency Lower Bound (Section III-E, Equation 4):**
   Section III-E states that *"The first window that contains the attack ends at most $\Delta$ after $\tau_a$, and $k$-of-$n$ confirmation adds $(k-1)\Delta$, so $D \ge (k - 1 + U)\Delta, \; U \in (0, 1]$."* Two unstated assumptions in this bound create an apparent contradiction with the rest of the paper:
   - *Omission of Fast-Path and Repeat-Offender Bypasses:* With the deployed parameters $\Delta = 2\text{ s}$ and $k = 2$, Eq. (4) implies $D > 2.0\text{ s}$ for all attacks. However, the proposed confirmation rule in Eq. (6) includes two single-window bypasses—the fast path ($s_d(t) \ge \eta_f$) and repeat-offender memory ($s_d(t) \ge \eta \wedge R_d(t) \neq \emptyset$)—both of which confirm an attack in the very first window ($k = 1$ effective delay, reducing the lower bound to $D \ge U\Delta \in (0, 2]\text{ s}$). Similarly, even under standard $k$-of-$n$ confirmation, if one of the $n - 1$ windows immediately preceding $\tau_a$ already had a benign fluctuation $s_d \ge \eta$, the first attack window suffices to reach $k = 2$ of $n = 3$. This explains why Table III reports mean detection latencies of $1.2\text{ s}$ (Mixed UDP+SYN) and $1.3\text{ s}$ (ICMP flood)—values strictly below the $2.0\text{ s}$ lower bound implied by Eq. (4).
   - *Sub-Window Accumulation at Small $U$:* In Eq. (4), $U\Delta \in (0, \Delta]$ represents the time between $\tau_a$ and the end of the first polling window overlapping the attack. If an attack starts infinitesimally before a poll ($U \to 0^+$), the partial window will not accumulate enough packets for $s_d(t)$ to cross $\eta$, pushing the first above-threshold window to the following poll and adding another $\Delta$. Explicitly scoping Eq. (4) to pure $k$-of-$n$ confirmation (with no pre-attack exceedances and sufficient first-window traffic to cross $\eta$) prior to introducing the fast path would resolve the apparent contradiction with Table III.

5. **Boundary Singularities and Undefined Terms in Level-2 Feature Definitions (Section III-B):**
   In the definition of the destination feature vector $\phi_d(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{19}$ and source-destination pair feature vector $\phi_{s,d}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$, several features can encounter division-by-zero or $\log(0)$ singularities during normal operation or lack explicit mathematical formulas:
   - *Normalized Source Entropy:* Defined as $H_d = -\sum_s q_s \log q_s / \log n_d$ with $q_s = \lambda_{s,d} / \Lambda_d$. Whenever a destination has only $n_d = 1$ active source in window $t$, $\log n_d = \log(1) = 0$ in the denominator, causing a $0/0$ singularity (and when $n_d = 0$, $\Lambda_d = 0$ leaves $q_s$ undefined).
   - *Response Ratio and Rate Change Features:* The response ratio $\rho_d$ (bytes from $d$ to its sources over bytes towards $d$) and the per-pair response ratio in $\phi_{s,d}(t)$ have a zero denominator if no bytes arrive at $d$ in a window (and similarly for flagged-flow/flagged-packet shares when active flows or packets are zero). Furthermore, "log rate change" in $\phi_d(t)$ and "rate jump" and "active windows" in $\phi_{s,d}(t)$ are listed by name only without algebraic formulas specifying whether "rate jump" is a difference or ratio relative to window $t-1$ (or to baseline $\mu_{s,d}$), whether "active windows" is a cumulative or sliding-window count, and how zero rates in $t-1$ are smoothed. Specifying the boundary conventions (e.g., setting $H_d = 0$ when $n_d \le 1$ and stating the smoothing constants) would ensure the feature extraction pipeline is unambiguously reproducible.

2. **Discrepancies Between Equation (7), the Prose, and Algorithm 1 in Source Attribution and Cold-Start / Evasion Edge Cases:**
   Several consistency and operational boundary conditions in the source attribution logic (Section IV-B and Algorithm 1) represent potential concerns:
   - **Omission of Multi-Window Persistence and Repeat-Offender Terms in Equation (7):** Equation (7) defines the block set $B_d(t)$ purely as a single-window predicate at time $t$:
     $$B_d(t) = \left\{ s : \lambda_{s,d} \ge \lambda_{\text{floor}} \land \lambda_{s,d} \ge \beta \mu_{s,d} \land \left( r_{s,d} \ge \eta_s \lor (\lambda_{s,d} \ge \lambda_{\text{rate}} \land \text{corr}(s)) \right) \right\}.$$
     However, the prose at the end of Section IV-B states that "a source enters $B_d$ only after qualifying in two consecutive windows (repeat offenders at once)." Defining Eq. (7) as an instantaneous candidate set $\tilde{B}_d(t)$ and writing the persistence-filtered block set as $B_d(t) = \big(\tilde{B}_d(t) \cap \tilde{B}_d(t-1)\big) \cup \big(\tilde{B}_d(t) \cap R_d(t)\big)$ would align the formal equation with the prose and Line 14 of Algorithm 1. Similarly, in Section IV-A and Eq. (6), $R_d(t)$ is defined in prose as sources blocked at $d$ within the last $M = 300\text{ s}$ that are "sending again," without specifying whether "sending again" means any packet ($\lambda_{s,d}(t) > 0$), exceeding the rate floor ($\lambda_{s,d}(t) \ge \lambda_{\text{floor}}$), or satisfying $\tilde{B}_d(t)$—a distinction that matters because $R_d(t) \neq \emptyset$ allows a single window with $s_d(t) \ge \eta$ to trigger confirmation in Eq. (6).
   - **Gating Attribution on $s_d(t) \ge \eta$ in Algorithm 1:** Line 13 of Algorithm 1 gates source attribution and rule installation on `if state_d \in {CONFIRMED, MITIGATING} and s_d >= \eta then`. During low-rate or pulsing attacks—or once initial drop rules begin suppressing traffic—$s_d(t)$ can dip below $\eta$ for one or two windows (fewer than $Q_e$ quiet windows, so the episode remains active). Requiring $s_d(t) \ge \eta$ on Line 13 prevents two-window persistence from completing if a source's second qualifying window coincides with $s_d(t) < \eta$.
   - **Cold-Start Baseline ($\mu_{s,d} = 0$) for Newly Arriving Legitimate High-Rate Flows and Slow-Ramp Evasion:** In Section IV-B, $\text{corr}(s)$ holds "if $s$ was never seen sending to $d$ in normal windows." If a legitimate client initiates a new bulk transfer or video stream to $d$ for the first time while $d$ is already under a confirmed attack, its pre-attack baseline is $\mu_{s,d} = 0$ (trivially satisfying $\lambda_{s,d} \ge \beta \mu_{s,d}$) and $\text{corr}(s)$ is automatically satisfied via the "never seen in normal windows" clause. Consequently, any newly arriving legitimate source sending at $\ge \lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\text{ pps}$ (such as the $2\text{ Mbit/s}$ bulk TCP transfers in Section V-B, which average $\approx 166\text{ pps}$ at a 1500-byte MTU) for two consecutive windows would be blocked, potentially violating the zero-collateral constraint $|B_v \cap L| = 0$. Conversely, because $\lambda_{s,d} \ge \beta \mu_{s,d}$ is a mandatory conjunction in Eq. (7) and $\mu_{s,d} \leftarrow \max(\lambda_{s,d}, 0.97\mu_{s,d})$ updates during normal windows ($s_d(t) < \eta$) and decays slowly ($\approx 45\text{ s}$ half-life), a small number of attackers that ramp up their traffic while the aggregate score remains $s_d(t) < \eta$ could inflate $\mu_{s,d}$ above $\frac{1}{\beta}$ of their flood rate and evade attribution even when $r_{s,d} \ge \eta_s$.

3. **Interaction Between Ingress Drop Rules, Counter Deltas, Rule Timeouts, and Anti-Spoofing Port Quarantine:**
   In Section IV-C and Algorithm 1, mitigation installs priority-200 per-source drop rules with `hard_timeout = 300 s` at the attacker's ingress switch (Line 15) and reactive port-quarantine rules `(in_port=p, ipv4_dst=d, drop)` when a host port emits $\ge \kappa = 20$ spoofed packet-ins towards $d$ in one window (Line 21). Several operational interactions warrant clarification for reproducibility:

1. **Victim Access Link Capacity Mismatch Between Calibration and Test Sessions (Sections IV-D and V-B):**
   In Section V-B (page 4), the testbed configuration specifies that *"The victim h200 sits behind a 25 Mbit/s access link in the test sessions (50 Mbit/s in calibration)."* Simultaneously, steady legitimate traffic delivers *"About 17 Mbit/s of benign load"* to the victim (including 10 bulk TCP transfers at 2 Mbit/s each), and flash crowds make all 95 web clients (60% targeting the victim) fetch a 100 kB page six times in 20 s (adding $\approx 95 \times 0.60 \times 6 \times 800\text{ kbit} / 20\text{ s} \approx 13.7\text{ Mbit/s}$ towards the victim, pushing total benign flash-crowd demand above $30\text{ Mbit/s}$). 1. **Test-Schedule Data Leakage and Adaptive Overfitting in Source Attribution Design (Sections VI-C, VI-D, VI-E, VII, and Table V)**
   - **Potential Methodological Concern:** A central claim in Section VI-C, Table IV, and Section VII (as well as the Abstract) is that the mitigation system blocks all 40 attackers while achieving **zero collateral damage** (*"no legitimate host was blocked,"* $|B_v \cap L| = 0$). Furthermore, in Table V, run `v1` was *"stopped after 5 of 14 segments"* (footnote $\S$), meaning its 9 blocked legitimate hosts occurred over roughly one-third of the schedule and are not evaluated over the same time horizon as the full 14-segment runs (`v2`–`final`).    - **Suggested Improvement:** Either evaluate the frozen `final` controller configuration on at least one genuinely unseen random seed (`seed 3`, with newly randomized client traffic timings, bitrates, attacker subsets, and attack orderings) to substantiate out-of-sample zero-collateral performance, or explicitly qualify the zero-collateral claim in Section VI-C, Section VII, and the Abstract to reflect that the attribution filters were iteratively tuned on the `seed 2` test schedule. 2. **Confounding of Continuous Test Schedules and Paired Per-Attack Mitigation Results by Sequential State Carryover and Unmitigated Control-Plane Collapse (Sections V-B, VI-C, Table IV, and Fig. 5)**
   - **Potential Concern (Causal Attribution Across Sequential Episodes):** The test schedule (Section V-B) and the paired evaluation between the unmitigated baseline (`test_det`, `test_det2`) and the mitigated run (`test_mit`) execute all nine attack episodes sequentially within a single continuous session separated by $40\text{ s}$ benign gaps (*"attacks last 30–105 s with 40 s gaps"*). Because several network and controller recovery timescales exceed $40\text{ s}$, running all episodes back-to-back without resetting switch and controller state introduces strong confounding effects across consecutive episodes:
     1. *Unrecovered control plane in unmitigated runs and apparent full recovery without active rule installation in Low-Rate UDP:* As reported in Section VI-C (page 5), in the unmitigated runs, *"from the ICMP flood onward legitimate service collapses and does not recover between attacks"* because the switch `packet-in` queues (rate-limited to $400\text{ pps}$ per switch) remain saturated. Consequently, during the six attack episodes following the third attack (ICMP)—namely `low-rate`, `pulsing`, `spoofed`, `mixed`, `protocol switching`, and `re-attack`—the $40\text{ s}$ inter-attack gaps do not return the network to a clean benign steady state ($H_0$), and in detection-only runs, the destination-centric window features $\phi_d(t)$ (such as active legitimate flows, byte response ratio $\rho_d$, and source entropy $H_d$) are evaluated over an already-collapsed background traffic distribution. In Table IV, the fourth attack episode (**Low-rate**, where attackers send $25\text{--}45\text{ pps}$) reports `0/40` new rules installed (`1st rule: –`, and unlike `Proto. switch` and `Re-attack`, it is not marked with footnote $\ddagger$), yet legitimate HTTP success is reported as rising from $0.06 \to 1.00$, UDP query success from $0.00 \to 1.00$, and Ping delivery from $0.34 \to 0.99$. This creates two unaddressed anomalies:
        - First, regarding why $0/40$ rules were installed: although Low-rate UDP is detected at the destination level (latency $5.1\text{ s}$ in Table III) and each attacker's rate ($25\text{--}45\text{ pps}$) exceeds the attribution floor $\lambda_{\text{floor}} = 15\text{ pps}$ (Eq. 7), individual attackers fall well below the rate-corroboration threshold $\lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\text{ pps}$ and evidently do not exceed the logistic source score threshold $\eta_s$. Thus, low-rate floods are **detected but not actively attributed/mitigated** ($0/40$ rules installed), making them a second unmitigated vector alongside ICMP floods.
        - Second, regarding why HTTP success jumps from $0.06 \to 1.00$ with $0$ new rules installed: the $0.06$ HTTP success rate during the unmitigated Low-rate episode is largely a carryover artifact of persistent control-plane collapse from earlier floods rather than congestion caused by the Low-rate traffic itself ($40 \times 35\text{ pps} \approx 1.4\text{ kpps}$, which does not saturate the $25\text{ Mbit/s}$ link or the $400\text{ pps/switch}$ `packet-in` limit). Conversely, in `test_mit`, the text does not explain how the network achieved $1.00$ HTTP success during Low-rate despite the immediately preceding ICMP flood only blocking $1/40$ attackers (e.g., whether the $38/40$ and $36/40$ all-protocol drop rules from the earlier UDP and TCP SYN episodes—which have a $300\text{ s}$ hard timeout—remained active across the ICMP and Low-rate episodes). Crediting the mitigation system with a $0.06 \to 1.00$ recovery on Low-rate UDP and folding it into the headline $48\% \to 89\%$ average across nine attacks conflates preventing earlier control-plane collapse (or residual rules from earlier floods) with actively mitigating the low-rate attack.
     2. *Hold-down timer ($Q_e \Delta = 48\text{ s}$), repeat-offender memory ($M = 300\text{ s}$), and $300\text{ s}$ rule-timeout carryover in Protocol Switching and Re-attack:* Section IV-A specifies that a destination's drop rules are removed after $Q_e = Q \cdot 2^{\min(e-1, 3)}$ quiet windows ($Q = 3$, $\Delta = 2\text{ s}$), while repeat-offender memory $R_d(t)$ persists for $M = 300\text{ s}$ and Section IV-C sets a $300\text{ s}$ hard timeout on OpenFlow rules. Once the episode count reaches $e \ge 4$, the hold-down duration reaches $24 \text{ windows} \times 2\text{ s} = 48\text{ s}$, which exceeds the $40\text{ s}$ benign gap between scheduled attacks. As noted by footnote $\ddagger$ in Table IV, during **Protocol switching** (`0/24` new rules installed) and **Re-attack** (`4/30` new rules installed), most attackers were already blocked prior to episode onset by rules carried over from the preceding **Mixed** episode ($34/40$ rules), and any unblocked returning attackers immediately trigger repeat-offender confirmation ($R_d(t) \neq \emptyset$). Therefore, the Protocol-switching episode only confirms that pre-existing all-protocol `ipv4_src`/`ipv4_dst` rules from an earlier episode did not expire during the $40\text{ s}$ gap; it does not test whether the controller can dynamically detect, attribute, and block an unblocked attacker switching protocols mid-episode.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Clarify in Section V-B how the 40 attackers are sampled or reused across consecutive episodes within a session, state in Section VI-C, Section VI-E, and the Table IV caption that Low-rate attacks are detected but not attributed (`0/40` rules installed), and explain why `test_mit` achieves $1.00$ HTTP success during the Low-rate episode despite `1/40` rules in ICMP and `0/40` in Low-rate. To isolate cold-start detection latency and per-attack mitigation capability from schedule-ordering and carryover artifacts, either report paired runs where switch flow tables, `packet-in` queues, and controller state ($R_d(t)$ and $e$) are reset before each attack type (or extend inter-attack gaps beyond $\max(Q_e \Delta, 300\text{ s})$), or explicitly separate active per-episode mitigation from carryover effects when computing summary averages in Table IV.

## Summary
This paper investigates DDoS detection and source-scoped mitigation in OpenFlow-based Software-Defined Networks (SDN), addressing the common deployment gap where flow-level machine learning classifiers trained on isolated public datasets fail to transfer across networks or protect legitimate traffic during live mitigation. The authors cast SDN DDoS defense as a decomposed two-stage problem—quickest change detection under a single-window false-alarm budget paired with goodput-maximizing source block-set selection under a flow-table budget and zero-collateral constraint—and implement a three-level Ryu controller architecture combining a multi-dataset flow classifier (Level 1), 19 destination-centric OpenFlow counter-delta window features (Level 2), and a calibrated logistic detector with $k$-of-$n$ plus fast-path confirmation, baseline-aware persistence-filtered attribution, and reactive port-quarantine anti-spoofing (Level 3). Evaluated on a 200-host, 25-switch Mininet/Open vSwitch testbed across nine attack types, fast-path-parity baselines, flow-ML and attribution ablations, link-capacity recalibration, and out-of-sample fresh-seed validation, the proposed system achieves the highest window F1 ($0.78$ on tuning seed 2; $0.73$ frozen and $0.78$ recalibrated on fresh seed 3) with $\approx 3.2\text{--}3.5\text{ s}$ mean detection latency and zero collateral blocks of legitimate hosts, while transparently documenting operational edge cases such as server-concentrated false positives and attribution blindness under control-plane `packet-in` saturation.

## Strengths
* **Rigorous Experimental Hygiene and Scientific Transparency:** The paper directly confronts well-known evaluation pitfalls in network security ML by demonstrating severe cross-dataset degradation between CIC-DDoS2019 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 ($0.42\text{--}0.47$ balanced accuracy), enforcing fast-path parity across baselines, conducting out-of-sample fresh-seed validation with frozen configurations, and candidly diagnosing negative results and telemetry artifacts (such as carry-over collapse and server role skew).
* **Effective Destination-Centric Feature Synthesis and Collateral-Free Scoping:** Aggregating per-switch OpenFlow counter deltas (with a counter-restart correction) into destination-centric window features and gating per-source drop rules via pre-attack peak-hold baselines ($\beta\mu_{s,d}$) and two-window persistence achieves 100% episode recall across all nine attack types while blocking zero legitimate hosts across both test seeds.
* **Clear Problem Decomposition and Diagnostic Ablations:** Separating the design constants of the confirmation state machine from the calibrated quantile thresholds and systematically ablating both the Level-1 flow classifier (showing it contributes $\approx 0.04$ F1 while Levels 2+3 drive the bulk of the performance) and the four successive versions of the attribution rule provide clear insight into where the architectural gains originate.

## Weaknesses
* **Theoretical Disconnect Between the Bit-Rate Mitigation Objective and Packet-Rate Attribution Ranking:** Please consider aligning the formal mitigation formulation in Equations (4)–(5) with the plug-in solver in Section III-E and Equation (9); whereas Equation (5) requires greedily blocking attackers with the largest offered bit-rate load ($\ell_{a,v} = \lambda_{a,v}\bar{b}_{a,v}$) and omits port quarantine $Q$ from legitimate goodput and the zero-collateral constraint, the controller selects and ranks candidates using packet-rate thresholds ($\lambda_{\text{floor}}, \lambda_{\text{rate}}$) and logistic probabilities ($r_{s,d}$) without weighting by mean packet size $\bar{b}_{s,v}$.
* **Pre-Split Label Snooping and Pooled Role Calibration Bias:** The authors might consider resolving two statistical evaluation issues: (1) removing identical feature vectors with conflicting labels across the *entire* flow datasets prior to the 80/20 split uses holdout test labels to filter ambiguous boundary flows, violating strict pre-split isolation (Reference Paper: Dos and Don'ts of Machine Learning in Computer Security, Daniel Arp et al., 2022), and (2) calibrating the window threshold $\eta$ by pooling benign windows across $\approx 97\%$ quiet clients and $1$ busy server satisfies the $0.3\%$ budget globally while inducing a $24.0\%\text{--}29.6\%$ benign window FPR on the victim server and $30\text{--}89$ false-alarm episodes per hour.
* **Fast-Path Threshold Inversion Under Link-Capacity Recalibration:** Treating the fast-path trigger $\eta_f = 0.97$ as a fixed constant while dynamically recalibrating $\eta$ causes a threshold inversion ($\eta = 0.980 > \eta_f = 0.97$) when recalibrated at the $25\text{ Mbit/s}$ test capacity, collapsing the intended 2-of-3 sequential confirmation state machine into an unbuffered 1-of-1 rule and surging false-alarm episodes from $5.8$ to $63$ per hour on Seed 3.
* **Counter-Based Attribution Blindness Under Control-Plane Saturation and Low-Rate Attacks:** While Level-2 detection incorporates `packet-in` rates, source attribution in Equation (9) relies exclusively on installed OpenFlow counters ($\lambda_{s,d}$) and hard packet-rate floors ($\ge 15\text{--}150\text{ pps}$); consequently, low-rate attackers evade attribution ($0/40$ attributed on seed 2), and volumetric floods on fresh seed 3 that saturate the $400\text{ pps}$ switch `packet-in` limit prevent flow setup, hiding 28 of 40 ICMP attackers from attribution and highlight the need for `packet-in`-based attribution or control-plane proxies (Reference Paper: FloodGuard: A DoS Attack Prevention Extension in Software-Defined Networks, Haopei Wang et al., 2015; Reference Paper: Combining OpenFlow and sFlow for an Effective and Scalable Anomaly Detection and Mitigation Mechanism on SDN Environments, K. Giotis et al., 2014).
* **Missing Sequential Change-Point Baseline (CUSUM) for the Confirmation Heuristic:** Because the paper formulates DDoS detection as quickest change detection under a false-alarm budget and notes that its 2-of-3 plus fast-path confirmation rule is a heuristic without formal optimality guarantees, the authors might consider evaluating a standard sequential CUSUM stopping rule offline on the logged window scores $s_d(t)$ to benchmark the delay-versus-false-alarm trade-off of their state machine (Reference Paper: Continuous Inspection Schemes, E. S. Page, 1954; Reference Paper: Procedures for Reacting to a Change in Distribution, G. Lorden, 1971).

#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Counter-Delta Piecewise Formulation in Equation (1) vs. Re-installed and Newly Seen Flow Entries (Section III-B, Page 2):**
   Equation (1) defines the per-switch packet counter delta for flow $f$ on switch $\sigma$ at window $t$ as:
   $$x_{f,\sigma}(t) = \begin{cases} P_{f,\sigma}(t) - P_{f,\sigma}(t - 1), & P_{f,\sigma}(t) \ge P_{f,\sigma}(t - 1) \\ P_{f,\sigma}(t)\min\!\left(1, \frac{e_\sigma(t)}{\delta_{f,\sigma}(t)}\right), & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$
   The accompanying prose specifies four operational cases: normal monotonic increments, re-installed entries with restarted counters (whose count is spread over their age $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t)$), re-added entries with continuous counters (age restarts, counter does not fall), and entries seen for the first time with $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) \le e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ (counting the whole counter). A potential concern is that because the mathematical branching condition in Equation (1) tests only whether $P_{f,\sigma}(t) \ge P_{f,\sigma}(t - 1)$, several boundary behaviors described in the prose and evaluated in the Section VI-E ablation are not captured by the formula:
   - *Entries absent at poll $t-1$ (newly seen flows or entries that timed out before $t-1$ and re-appeared at $t$):* Equation (1) does not define $P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ when flow $f$ was absent from switch $\sigma$'s flow table at poll $t-1$. If $P_{f,\sigma}(t-1) = 0$ by convention, then $P_{f,\sigma}(t) \ge P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ holds trivially for every newly observed flow, routing it to the first branch ($x_{f,\sigma}(t) = P_{f,\sigma}(t)$) regardless of whether $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) \le e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ or $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) > e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ (whereas the prose and the Section VI-E rate-spike correction imply that an entry appearing with $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) > e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ is meant to be routed to the second, age-prorated branch). Conversely, if $P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ caches the last seen counter before expiration and a flow is re-installed between poll $t-1$ and poll $t$, its new age satisfies $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) \le e_\sigma(t)$, which makes $\min(1, e_\sigma(t)/\delta_{f,\sigma}(t)) = 1$ unless the entry had already been absent for at least one poll.
   - *Re-installed entries whose restarted counter exceeds the prior entry's counter:* If a flow entry had a small cumulative counter $P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ before timing out, is re-installed from scratch, and accumulates a burst $P_{f,\sigma}(t) \ge P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ by the next poll, testing only $P_{f,\sigma}(t) \ge P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ routes the flow to the first branch and erroneously subtracts the old, expired entry's counter $P_{f,\sigma}(t-1)$ from the new entry's counter rather than treating it as a restarted counter.
   - *Zero-age boundary ($\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) = 0$):* In OpenFlow `OFPMP_FLOW_STATS`, if duration is evaluated at $0$ when an entry is polled immediately upon installation (e.g., if integer `duration_sec` is used), the ratio $e_\sigma(t)/\delta_{f,\sigma}(t)$ in the second branch divides by zero unless guarded by the $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) \le e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ condition first or lower-bounded via $\max(\delta_{f,\sigma}(t), \epsilon)$. Explicitly incorporating prior-poll presence and the age condition $\delta_{f,\sigma}(t) \le e_\sigma(t) + 1\,\text{s}$ into the piecewise conditions of Equation (1) would make the telemetry model self-contained and exact.

2. **Port Quarantine ($Q$) Collateral Damage, Equivalence Scope, and Candidate Ranking in the Mitigation Problem (Section III-E, Equations 4–5, Page 3):**
   There are three potential discrepancies between the formal block-set optimization problem in Equations (4)–(5) and the actual mitigation mechanics described in Sections III-E and IV:
   - *Omission of $Q$ from legitimate goodput and the zero-collateral constraint:* In Equation (4), the port-quarantine exclusion superscript $Q$ (*"$\ell^Q$ excludes traffic entering through $Q$"*) is applied only to the attacker sum $\sum_{a \in A \setminus B} \ell_{a,v}^Q$, leaving the legitimate offered loads $\ell_{s,v}$ and $\sum_{u \in L} \ell_{u,v}$ independent of $Q$, and the zero-collateral constraint in Equation (5) is written solely as $|B \cap L| = 0$. Because Section IV-C specifies that a port quarantine drops all traffic toward $d$ entering port $(\sigma, p)$ with no allow rule for the bound address $\beta(\sigma, p)$, quarantining a legitimate host's port (or a shared/NAT port, as discussed in Section VI-G) reduces that legitimate host's goodput to zero. As written in Equations (4)–(5), placing legitimate ports into $Q$ incurs no mathematical goodput penalty and violates no constraint unless $\ell_{s,v}^Q$ is also used for $s \in L$ and $Q$ is constrained not to contain ports bound to legitimate hosts (e.g., $\{\beta(\sigma, p) : (\sigma, p) \in Q\} \cap L = \emptyset$).
   - *Bit-rate minimization objective vs. logistic source-score ranking in the plug-in solver:* Below Equation (5), the text states that maximizing $\sum_{s \in L} G_{s,v}(B, Q)$ *"is equivalent to minimising the residual attack load $\sum_{a \in A \setminus B} \ell_{a,v}^Q$ under the same constraints: block the heaviest attackers first..."* First, because $\min(1, C_v / (\dots))$ in Equation (4) saturates at $1$ once total unblocked load falls at or below $C_v$, minimizing residual attack load is a *sufficient* condition for maximizing Equation (5) (and is strictly equivalent only while the bottleneck link remains overloaded). Second, blocking the heaviest attackers first to minimize residual offered bit rate $\sum_{a \in A \setminus B} \ell_{a,v}^Q$ under a rule cap requires ranking candidates by their offered *bit rate* $\ell_{s,v} = \lambda_{s,v}\bar{b}_{s,v}$, whereas the plug-in solver described immediately afterwards ranks the attributed set *"by source score (rate-rule hits first)"* (i.e., by the logistic classification probability $r_{s,d}(t)$ across 10 features and the packet-rate threshold $\lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\,\text{pps}$ rather than bit rate $\ell_{s,v}$).
   - *Combined vs. separate rule budgets:* Equation (5) places a joint budget $|B| + |Q| \le R_{\max}$ on per-source blocks and port quarantines, whereas the controller caps per-source drop rules $B_d(t)$ at $R_{\max} = 64$ and installs port-quarantine rules $Q$ independently (Algorithm 1, lines 20 and 26). 4. **Rate Distortion from Path-Maximum Delta Selection and Global Maximum Reply-Interval Normalization $\bar{e}(t)$ (Section III-B, Page 2):**
   In Section III-B, the per-switch delta $x_{f,\sigma}(t)$ accrues over switch $\sigma$'s local inter-reply interval $e_\sigma(t)$ (and in the second branch of Equation 1 is explicitly scaled by $e_\sigma(t)/\delta_{f,\sigma}(t)$). ### [Flow-Level Data Pipeline, Testbed Design, Baselines & Cross-Dataset Evaluation] (Pages: 4-6)

1. **Pre-Split Label-Conflict Removal, Cross-Split Feature Duplication, and Grouping Granularity in Flow-Level Data (Section V-A, Page 4)**
   - **Definitive Methodological Issue (Pre-Split Label Snooping):** Section V-A states that *"Rows whose feature vectors are identical but whose labels differ are removed over each whole dataset before splitting (this uses all labels, including those later in the holdout); identical rows with the same label are kept."* Although transparently disclosed, filtering conflicting-label rows across the entire dataset prior to the 80/20 train/test split constitutes transductive data snooping (specifically violating the pre-split isolation principle of Arp et al. [7], which is cited in Section II as the motivation for the evaluation design). In a real network deployment, a flow classifier encounters raw traffic where benign and malicious flows may project to identical coarse 7-element lifetime vectors $z_f \in \mathbb{R}^7$ (`protocol`, `duration`, `packets`, `bytes`, `packet rate`, `byte rate`, `mean packet size`)—such as single-packet UDP queries or zero-duration TCP SYN probes—without an oracle to filter out conflicting instances beforehand. Eliminating these ambiguous boundary instances from the holdout set using ground-truth test labels removes irreducible Bayes error and artificially inflates the within-dataset holdout balanced accuracies reported in Figure 2 and Table II.
   - **Potential Concern (Cross-Split Feature Duplicates and Minute-Level Grouping):** Because identical 7-feature vectors with the *same* label are retained, grouping by bidirectional 5-tuple with protocol (for CIC-DDoS2019) or by `day × capture minute × destination port` (for CSE-CIC-IDS2018) does not prevent identical 7-tuple feature vectors generated by the same attack tool or standard client queries across different 5-tuples or different capture minutes from appearing simultaneously in both the training and holdout splits. Furthermore, in CSE-CIC-IDS2018 (where IP addresses are absent), DoS and DDoS attack campaigns span many consecutive minutes on the same day targeting the same destination port (e.g., port 80); grouping at the granularity of individual capture minutes allows adjacent minutes ($t$ and $t+1$) of the same continuous attack episode to straddle the training and holdout sets.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Resolve label conflicts strictly within the training split while leaving the holdout split unmodified to reflect true test-time ambiguity. Additionally, report holdout balanced accuracy both with and without exact cross-split $z_f$ duplicates removed relative to the training set, and for CSE-CIC-IDS2018, consider grouping by contiguous time blocks or distinct attack sessions rather than individual capture minutes.

   - **Potential Concern:** In Section V-A, the Level-1 flow classifier $g_\theta(z_f)$ is trained on flow records from CIC-DDoS2019 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018, where each flow represents a bidirectional 5-tuple transport connection (including TCP/UDP source and destination ports). In Section V-B, however, the Mininet/Open vSwitch testbed installs forwarding entries *"per (source, destination, protocol)"* with a 15 s idle timeout. Under unidirectional 3-tuple `(source, destination, protocol)` matching, all concurrent and sequential transport connections from host $s$ to destination $d$ over a given IP protocol (for instance, an `hping3` SYN or UDP flood randomizing source ports, or a web client fetching multiple HTTP pages) are aggregated into a single OpenFlow switch entry whose `duration`, `packets`, and `bytes` accumulate continuously until a 15 s idle gap occurs.
   - **Impact:** This structural domain shift between 5-tuple bidirectional connection records in training and 3-tuple unidirectional host-pair aggregates at test time has two important downstream consequences:
     1. It shifts the test-time feature distribution $z_f$ away from the training distribution, which helps explain why the `Flow-ML only` baseline misses all TCP SYN and ICMP floods (Tables IV and VI) and why Level 1 contributes only ~0.04 F1 to the overall hybrid detector.
     2. Because a source-destination pair $(s, d)$ can have at most one active forwarding entry per IP protocol at a time (at most three across TCP, UDP, and ICMP), per-pair flow-count features in Table I (`flows`, `new flows`, `packets/flow`) collapse to small protocol counts rather than connection counts, and the source-attribution corroboration rule in Section IV-B (*"$s$ opens $\ge 10$ new flows to $d$ in the window"*) cannot be triggered by a single source within a 2 s polling window unless its 3-tuple entry is repeatedly evicted and re-installed multiple times per second.

### [End-to-End Detection, Source-Scoped Mitigation, Ablations & Claims Verification] (Pages: 5-9)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Statistical Validity of Pooled Threshold Calibration and Extreme Role-Based False-Positive Concentration (Sections VI-B, VI-F, VI-G, VII; Tables III, V, VI, X)**
   - **Definitive Methodological Limitation and Potential Concern:** The formal detection problem in Eq. (3) defines the constraint $\Pr(s_d(t) \ge \eta \mid H_0) \le \varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon = 0.3\%$) *"For every destination and window"* (Section III-C). However, because the calibration procedure (Section IV-D) pools benign windows across all active destinations, approximately $96.5\%\text{--}97\%$ of the empirical distribution consists of quiet client hosts ($22{,}482$ client windows vs. $223$ victim server windows in calibration; $37{,}267\text{--}37{,}788$ client windows vs. $318\text{--}325$ victim server windows in test sessions, Table V). Consequently, the pooled $(1-\varepsilon)$ quantile sets $\eta$ near the upper tail of benign client traffic rather than server traffic. While the pooled network-wide window FPR stays near $0.26\%\text{--}0.30\%$ ($0.0\%$ on clients), the formal per-destination $\varepsilon = 0.3\%$ constraint is violated on the primary defended target by nearly two orders of magnitude: the victim server (`h200`) experiences a **$24.0\%\text{--}29.6\%$ benign window false-positive rate** (Table V), driving **$30\text{--}89$ false-alarm episodes per hour** across the network (Table III).
   - **Actionable Improvement:** Although the authors candidly highlight this role effect in the Abstract, Section VI-B, Table V, and Section VI-G and note per-role calibration as future work, deferring stratified calibration leaves it unclear whether the $24\%\text{--}28\%$ post-attack server FPR is merely an artifact of pooling heterogeneous roles or an intrinsic limitation of the Level-2 window features during post-attack recovery and flash crowds. Because the window telemetry from the calibration (`seed 1`) and test sessions (`seed 2`, `seed 3`) is already logged and replayed offline in Section VI-F, evaluating a simple stratified threshold offline (e.g., calibrating $\eta_{\text{server}}$ on the $884$ benign server windows from `h198`–`h200` in Table V and $\eta_{\text{client}}$ on the client windows, or normalizing destination rate/count features in $\phi_d(t)$ against a per-destination baseline analogous to $\mu_{s,d}$ at the source level) would directly verify whether enforcing the $0.3\%$ budget on busy servers preserves detection recall and low latency.

2. **Interaction of Bot Reuse, Inter-Attack Gaps, and Post-Attack False Positives with Hold-Down Timers, Repeat-Offender Memory, and Confounded Mitigation Claims (Algorithm 1, Sections V-B, VI-C, VI-D, VII; Tables V, VII, VIII)**
   - **Potential Experimental Design and Causal Attribution Concern:** In both the seed-2 ($40\text{ s}$ gaps, Table VIII) and seed-3 ($60\text{ s}$ gaps, Table VII) schedules, all nine consecutive attack episodes draw participants from the same fixed pool of $40$ bots, while installed OpenFlow drop rules match `(ipv4_src=s, ipv4_dst=d)` across all IP protocols with a $300\text{ s}$ `hard_timeout`. Under Algorithm 1 (Lines 11–18), returning from `MITIGATING` to `NORMAL` requires $Q_{e_d} = 3 \cdot 2^{\min(e_d - 1, 3)}$ consecutive quiet windows ($o_d(t) = 0$) to enter `RECOVERING` ($6\text{ s}, 12\text{ s}, 24\text{ s}, 48\text{ s}$ for $e_d \ge 1$, where $e_d$ is never reset), plus 3 additional quiet windows ($6\text{ s}$) in `RECOVERING`—requiring up to $(24 + 3) \times 2\text{ s} = 54\text{ s}$ of uninterrupted quiet windows once $e_d \ge 4$ (note that Section V-B states that $60\text{ s}$ is *"longer than the 48 s maximum hold-down"*, omitting the $6\text{ s}$ spent in `RECOVERING`). Because $24.0\%\text{--}28.3\%$ of the victim server's benign windows within $30\text{ s}$ after an attack exceed $\eta$ ($o_d(t) = 1$, Section VI-B) or residual drop-rule hits $\text{drop}_d(t) \ge 30\text{ pps}$ occur, sporadic post-attack false positives repeatedly reset `quiet_d` $\leftarrow 0$ during the inter-attack gaps and prevent rule removal:
     1. *Fresh Seed 3 Carry-Over Across $60\text{ s}$ Gaps:* Although the inter-attack gaps on seed 3 were specifically increased to $60\text{ s}$ to exceed the hold-down (Section V-B, p. 5), Table VII shows that in the `Low-rate` episode (immediately following `TCP SYN`), **`34` of the `40` attackers were still pre-blocked at onset** (`New / active: 2 / 34 of 40`), and **`7` of `24` were pre-blocked** in `Proto. switch` (`15 / 7 of 24`). While Section VI-D transparently explains that the seed-2 `Low-rate` HTTP improvement ($0.06 \to 1.00$ with $0/40$ bots attributed) is due to avoiding carry-over control-plane collapse rather than active mitigation, Section VI-C (p. 6) claims that on seed 3 *"Low-rate, re-attack and spoofed episodes recover almost fully."* In fact, just as on seed 2, the attribution layer is structurally unable to attribute low-rate bots ($25\text{--}45\text{ pps}$ per bot falls well below $\lambda_{\text{rate}} = 150\text{ pps}$ and the $\varepsilon_s = 1\%$ source score threshold $\eta_s$, attributing only $2$ new bots on seed 3); the seed-3 `Low-rate` recovery ($0.07 \to 0.95$ HTTP success) is primarily an artifact of $34/40$ bots remaining blocked from the preceding `TCP SYN` flood because post-attack false alarms prevented `quiet_d` from reaching $Q_{e_d}$.
     2. *Seed 2 Carry-Over, Repeat-Offender Memory ($M = 300\text{ s}$), and Cold-Start Latency:* On seed 2 (Table VIII), even after the second attack (`TCP SYN`, where the nominal hold-down is $Q_2 = 6\text{ windows} = 12\text{ s}$), rules fail to clear across the $40\text{ s}$ gap, leaving $36/40$ `ICMP`, $23/24$ `Proto. switch`, and $26/30$ `Re-attack` bots already blocked at onset, while the unprotected baseline suffers permanent control-plane collapse from the third attack (`ICMP`) onward. Furthermore, even when rules are removed during the $60\text{ s}$ gap on seed 3, the repeat-offender memory $R_d(t)$ retains any source blocked within the last $M = 300\text{ s}$ (Section IV-A); reusing the same 40 bot IPs across sequential episodes separated by $40\text{--}60\text{ s}$ means that attackers blocked in episode $i$ remain in $R_d(t)$ when episode $i+1$ (of a different attack vector) begins, triggering immediate single-window confirmation via Eq. (8), bypassing the 2-window persistence filter in Eq. (10), and making the `"1st rule"` latencies in Tables VII and VIII reflect warm-start repeat-offender blocking rather than cold-start mitigation latency. To isolate per-attack-type detection and mitigation performance in future runs, consider assigning disjoint attacker IP pools to distinct attack episodes (reserving bot reuse specifically for the designated "re-attack" episode) or resetting controller state ($e_d, R_d(t)$) and switch flow tables between distinct scheduled attacks.

3. **Incomplete Validation of the Recommended $25\text{ Mbit/s}$ Recalibration Without Fast-Path Degeneracy (Section VI-F, Table X)**
   - **Incomplete Validation Concern:** In Section VI-F (p. 8), recalibrating at the actual $25\text{ Mbit/s}$ test link capacity causes the base 2-of-3 window threshold to rise from $\eta = 0.844$ to $\eta = 0.980 > \eta_f = 0.97$, collapsing Eq. (8) into 1-of-1 confirmation and spiking false-alarm episodes from $25$ to $49\text{ FA/h}$ on seed 2 and from $5.8$ to $63\text{ FA/h}$ on seed 3. The authors accurately diagnose this degeneracy in Section VI-F, noting that *"the fast-path threshold must be set relative to $\eta$"* and concluding that *"the $25\text{ Mbit/s}$ calibration is the configuration we recommend."* However, Table X (p. 9) only evaluates `Proposed: 2-of-3 confirmation` (dropping the fast path and reporting only single-window F1 and FPR, which do not depend on confirmation), omitting episode detection latency and `FA/h`.
   - **Actionable Improvement:** Since Section V-C already defines a quantile-based fast-path rule for baseline parity (matching the $0.24\%$ calibration benign exceedance rate, i.e., $\varepsilon_f < \varepsilon = 0.3\%$), recalibrating $\eta_f$ to a stricter tail quantile at $25\text{ Mbit/s}$ (so $\eta_f > 0.980$) and reporting the resulting episode latency and `FA/h` in Table X would demonstrate that the recommended configuration operates with its fast path intact without suffering 1-of-1 degeneracy.

4. **Unexplained Latency Inversion in the Flow-ML Ablation (`Levels 2+3 only`) and Inconsistent Fast-Path Reporting Across Tables III, IV, and VI (Sections V-C, VI-B, VI-C; Tables III, IV, VI)**
   - **Definitive Discrepancy / Missing Explanation in Ablation Analysis:** In Table III (p. 6), removing the Level-1 flow classifier (`Levels 2+3 only (no flow ML)`, which the footnote notes *"uses its own fast path"*) lowers single-window F1 from $0.78$ to $0.73$, but achieves a **faster** mean and penalized episode confirmation latency (**$2.6\text{ s} / 2.6\text{ s}$**) than the full `Proposed + fast path` detector (**$3.2\text{ s} / 3.2\text{ s}$**) while detecting all **$18/18$** episodes at the exact same episode false-alarm rate (**$45\text{ FA/h}$**). Section VI-B discusses the drop in window F1 and the $21\%$ increase in false-positive windows ($0.26\% \to 0.32\%$ FPR), but never explains how the fast-path threshold for `Levels 2+3 only` was calibrated or why removing Level 1 reduces episode detection delay $\mathbb{E}_{\text{episodes}}[D]$ (the core objective of Eq. (3)) by $0.6\text{ s}$ ($19\%$ faster) at no cost in `FA/h`. Furthermore, in Table VI (seed 3, p. 7), the episode-level columns (`Det.` and `Lat./Pen.`) for `Levels 2+3 only` are left blank, and `FA/h` is omitted from Table VI and Table X, preventing out-of-sample verification of this latency behavior.
   - **Inconsistent Fast-Path Parity and Episode Counts in Tables IV and VI:** Although Section V-C gives every baseline a calibrated fast path for latency parity, Table IV (p. 7, captioned `2-OF-3, PROPOSED WITH FAST PATH`) compares `Prop.` *with* fast path (mean of the 9 rows $= 3.17\text{ s} \approx 3.2\text{ s}$, $18/18$ detected) against the four baselines *without* fast path (means of $5.4\text{ s}$, $7.5\text{ s}$, $6.9\text{ s}$, and $7.7\text{ s}$, matching the non-fast-path rows of Table III rather than the `+ fast path` rows where `DstEnt` and `pps` detect $18/18$ at $4.5\text{ s}$ and $5.3\text{ s}$). Moreover, for `SrcEnt` in Table IV, two attack types are marked `–` ("not detected" in either of the two seed-2 sessions: `TCP SYN flood` and `Protocol switching`) and one is marked `4.7†` ("detected in one of the two sessions": `Spoofed`), which implies at least $2 + 2 + 1 = 5$ missed episodes (at most $13/18$ detected), contradicting the `16/18` reported for `Source-IP entropy` in Table III. Similarly, Table VI includes a `+ fast path` row for `Dest.-IP entropy` and `Proposed + fast path`, but omits `+ fast path` rows for the other three baselines.

5. **Arithmetic/Rounding Discrepancies in Seed-3 Reporting and Ambiguity in "Attacker Coverage" (Sections VI-C, VI-E; Tables VI, VII)**
   - **Definitive Arithmetic Discrepancy in Seed-3 ICMP Attacker Counts (Section VI-C, p. 6):** When diagnosing the seed-3 ICMP flood, Section VI-C (p. 6, right column) states: *"In the ICMP episode the first rule came 22.9 s after onset and only 14 of 40 attackers were blocked: for 28 attackers the controller saw no flow counters at all in the confirmed windows, because the flood saturated the packet-in path and their forwarding entries were never installed."* Because there are $40$ attackers in total and attribution requires flow counters, $14$ blocked attackers plus $28$ attackers without flow counters sums to $42 > 40$. Please clarify whether $26$ (rather than $28$) unblocked attackers lacked flow counters, or whether $2$ of the $28$ attackers lacking counters in certain confirmed windows were eventually blocked before the episode ended.
   - **Definitive F1 Rounding Discrepancy in Table VI (`Flow-ML only`, p. 7):** Table VI reports `Flow-ML only` on seed 3 with $\text{Rec.} = 0.45$, $\text{FPR} = 0.22\%$, and $\text{F1} = 0.48$ across $34{,}898$ destination windows ($191$ under attack, $34{,}707$ benign). However, any integer true-positive count rounding to $\text{Rec.} = 0.45$ ($\text{TP} = 86$, $86/191 = 0.4503$) and false-positive count rounding to $\text{FPR} = 0.22\%$ ($\text{FP} \in \{75, 76, 77, 78\}$) yields precision $\text{Prec.} \in [0.524, 0.534]$ and harmonic mean $\text{F1} = 2\cdot\text{TP}/(2\cdot\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN}) \in [0.485, 0.489]$, which rounds to **$0.49$** rather than $0.48$. Adding a `Prec.` column to Table VI (matching Table III) and verifying the underlying window counts would resolve this discrepancy.
   - **Ambiguity in "Attacker Coverage" Definition (Section VI-E, p. 8):** At the end of Section VI-E (p. 8, left column), discussing the offline replay of the attribution rules on the seed-2 detection-only session, the text states: *"Attacker coverage stayed at 97–98 %."* Because $0/40$ attackers are attributed during the seed-2 low-rate episode (Section VI-D, Table VIII), the text should explicitly specify that $97\text{--}98\%$ refers to the fraction of unique bots ($39/40 = 97.5\%$) attributed at least once across the entire multi-attack session (or coverage across high-rate episodes only) rather than per-episode attribution recall.
